The response · Q 5:44–48

The Islamic Dilemma, answered in full

The argument needs the Qur’an to hold one of two positions about the Bible. Read in Arabic and in context, it holds neither.

Editorial team · 14 min read · Revision 1

The argument known as the Islamic Dilemma is short, which is much of its appeal. The Qur’an, it says, affirms the Torah and the Gospel as revelation. Either Christians in the Prophet’s time possessed those books intact, in which case the Qur’an is contradicted by them, or they did not, in which case the Qur’an was wrong to commend them. Both paths, it concludes, end with Islam refuted.

Of its five steps (set out on the argument page), the first is true. The second and fourth are false, and the third does not follow from the verse it cites. This reply takes each in turn.

1. What the Qur’an affirms Q 3:3–4

The Qur’an says God “sent down the Torah and the Gospel before, as guidance for people” Q 3:3–4. Muslims accept this without reservation; belief in the earlier books is an article of faith.1 The dispute is over what those names refer to, and what state the Qur’an says the books were in.

2. One Injil, given to Jesus Q 5:46

The Qur’an describes the Injil as something God gave to Jesus: “We gave him the Gospel, in which is guidance and light” Q 5:46. It is a revelation received by a prophet, as the Torah was given to Moses and the Qur’an to Muhammad.

The New Testament does not describe itself this way. Its four Gospels are accounts of Jesus written by others in the decades after him, and the other twenty-three books are letters, a history and an apocalypse. Mark’s opening line, “the beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ,” uses euangelion for the message Jesus preached, which is close to the Qur’anic sense. A book about Jesus is a different thing from the revelation given to him. The second premise treats them as identical, and the dilemma depends on that move.

3. “Let them judge by what God revealed therein” Q 5:47

وَلْيَحْكُمْ أَهْلُ الْإِنجِيلِ بِمَا أَنزَلَ اللَّهُ فِيهِ

“Let the people of the Gospel judge by what God has revealed therein.”

Q 5:47

The command is to judge by mā anzala Allāhu fīhi, “what God revealed in it.” It points to revelation that remains in what they hold. It says nothing about whether everything they hold is revelation. A Muslim can urge a Christian to follow Jesus’ command to love God with all one’s heart without vouching for every verse of the New Testament.

The surrounding passage rules out the stronger reading. A few verses earlier the Qur’an describes some of the same communities as people “who distort words from their places” Q 5:41, and earlier in the same surah it says of the Christians that “they forgot a portion of what they were reminded of” Q 5:14. A text that accuses a community of distortion in the same passage cannot be certifying that community’s copy of its scripture as flawless.

The Gospels contain the same pattern. Jesus tells his hearers to obey the teachers of the Law and, in the same speech, condemns them:

“The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses’ seat, so do and observe whatever they tell you, but not the works they do.”Matthew 23:2–3 · ESV

Nobody concludes from this that Jesus thought the scribes were infallible. The Qur’an is reasoning the same way.

4. Entrusted, not guarded Q 5:44 · 15:9

The fourth premise assumes the Qur’an promises to preserve every revealed book. It does not. Of the Torah, it says the rabbis and scholars judged

بِمَا اسْتُحْفِظُوا مِن كِتَابِ اللَّهِ وَكَانُوا عَلَيْهِ شُهَدَاءَ

“by what they were entrusted to preserve of the Book of God, and they were witnesses to it.”

Q 5:44

The verb istuḥfiẓū is passive: they were asked to keep it. Preservation was a duty placed on people. Of the Qur’an alone the language changes, and God takes the duty on Himself:

إِنَّا نَحْنُ نَزَّلْنَا الذِّكْرَ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَحَافِظُونَ

“It is We who sent down the Reminder, and it is We who will guard it.”

Q 15:9

A trust held by people can be broken by people, and the Qur’an says that in some places it was Q 2:79. That is not God failing. In the Qur’an’s own account, it is why a final revelation was sent with a different guarantee.2 As for “none can change His words” Q 6:115, the commentators read it as God’s decrees and promises, which no one can overturn, not as a statement about manuscripts.

5. The position the dilemma leaves out Q 5:48

وَأَنزَلْنَا إِلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ بِالْحَقِّ مُصَدِّقًا لِّمَا بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ مِنَ الْكِتَابِ وَمُهَيْمِنًا عَلَيْهِ

“We have sent down to you the Book in truth, confirming the scripture that came before it and as a guardian over it.”

Q 5:48

The Qur’an gives itself two roles toward earlier scripture. It is muṣaddiq, confirming, and muhaymin, a guardian or judge that decides what in the earlier record is authentic.3 Both roles only make sense if earlier scripture survives in part. If it were wholly intact there would be nothing to guard; if wholly lost, nothing to confirm.

That third option is the Qur’an’s stated view, not a later rescue. It was also the view of classical scholars. Ibn Taymiyyah’s al-Jawāb al-Ṣaḥīḥ, a fourteenth-century reply to a Christian treatise, discusses whether the alteration was of wording, of meaning or both, and holds that both occurred in places while much of the text remained.4

Conclusion. The dilemma offers two options: wholly preserved or wholly corrupted. The Qur’an holds a third, partly preserved and judged by the final revelation, and says so in the same passage the argument quotes. A dilemma that leaves out its opponent’s actual position does not refute it.
