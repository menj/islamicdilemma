!lead The Islamic Dilemma's strongest-sounding question is this: if the Gospel had been corrupted, why would God command Christians to judge by it and tell them they stand on nothing until they uphold it? A God who knew the book was unreliable, the argument says, would have told them to abandon it. The question deserves a full answer, and the answer lies in the passage these verses belong to.

## The passage, from verse 41 to verse 50 {#passage}

All three verses come from Sūrat al-Mā'idah, the fifth surah, and two of them from a single tightly connected passage. It is worth reading the passage in order.

It opens with the Prophet being saddened by people who "hasten into disbelief", among them some of the Jews of Medina who listen eagerly to falsehood and "change the words from their (right) times and places" {Q 5:41}. It describes them coming to the Prophet for a ruling and tells him he may judge between them or turn away, but if he judges, to judge with justice {Q 5:42}. Then it asks a pointed question:

::ayah Q 5:43 | وَكَيْفَ يُحَكِّمُونَكَ وَعِندَهُمُ ٱلتَّوْرَىٰةُ فِيهَا حُكْمُ ٱللَّهِ ثُمَّ يَتَوَلَّوْنَ مِنۢ بَعْدِ ذَٰلِكَ ۚ | But why do they come to thee for decision, when they have (their own) law before them?—therein is the (plain) command of Allah; yet even after that, they would turn away.

The passage goes on to describe the Torah as "therein was guidance and light", by which the prophets and rabbis judged, because "to them was entrusted the protection of Allah's book" {Q 5:44}. It describes the Gospel given to Jesus, also "guidance and light" {Q 5:46}.

It says: "Let the people of the Gospel judge by what Allah hath revealed therein" {Q 5:47}. And then it turns to the Qur'an, sent "as a guardian over" the earlier scripture, and tells the Prophet to judge between them by what God sent down to him and not to follow their desires {Q 5:48–49}.

Read in order, the passage does not certify the books in people's hands. It accuses some of them of distortion, rebukes them for evading rulings their own scripture contains, and ends by making the Qur'an the final standard.

## A case the passage was revealed about {#case}

The hadith collections record an incident that the commentators connect with this passage. A Jewish man and woman in Medina were accused of adultery, and some of their community brought the case to the Prophet, hoping for a lighter ruling than the one in their law.

The Prophet asked what the Torah said. When the Torah was brought and read, the reader placed his hand over the verse prescribing stoning; ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām, a former rabbi, told him to lift his hand, and the verse was there beneath it.[1]

The Torah does prescribe death for adultery {Deut 22:22}, and stoning in the case described in the verses that follow it. The incident illustrates the Qur'an's position exactly.

God's ruling was still present in the Torah, which is why the Qur'an can say "they have the Torah in which is God's ruling". And some of those who held it were concealing what it said. Both halves of the Qur'an's account, preservation in part and concealment in part, appear in one event.

## Q 5:47: two readings, one meaning {#two-readings}

::ayah Q 5:47 | وَلْيَحْكُمْ أَهْلُ ٱلْإِنجِيلِ بِمَآ أَنزَلَ ٱللَّهُ فِيهِ ۚ | Let the people of the Gospel judge by what Allah hath revealed therein.

Most of the canonical readings of the Qur'an read this verse as a command: *wa-l-yaḥkum*, "and let them judge". But a group of the readers of Kufa read *wa-li-yaḥkuma*, "and so that they would judge", and al-Ṭabarī counts both readings as well known and close in meaning.[2]

On that reading the verse continues the sentence before it: God gave Jesus the Gospel, with guidance and light, *so that* the people of the Gospel would judge by what God revealed in it. It describes the purpose for which the Gospel was given to Jesus' followers, not a command issued to seventh-century Christians about the books they then held.

Both readings are part of the Qur'an as Muslims have transmitted it, and they complement each other. On either reading, the verse speaks of "what God revealed therein", the revelation, which excludes whatever a later community bound together and called by the same name.

And on the command reading, the commentators point out that judging by what God revealed in the Gospel would include judging by its description of a coming prophet {Q 7:157}. A Christian who did that would be led to the Qur'an, which is why the next verse turns to it.

## Q 5:66: "what was sent down to them from their Lord" {#5-66}

::ayah Q 5:66 | وَلَوْ أَنَّهُمْ أَقَامُوا۟ ٱلتَّوْرَىٰةَ وَٱلْإِنجِيلَ وَمَآ أُنزِلَ إِلَيْهِم مِّن رَّبِّهِمْ لَأَكَلُوا۟ مِن فَوْقِهِمْ وَمِن تَحْتِ أَرْجُلِهِم ۚ | If only they had stood fast by the Law, the Gospel, and all the revelation that was sent to them from their Lord, they would have enjoyed happiness from every side.

The verb is *aqāmū*, from *iqāmah*, which means to establish, to put into practice, to stand by. It is the same word used for establishing prayer.

To uphold the Torah and the Gospel, in this sense, is to live by what God revealed in them. No one has to declare every manuscript accurate.

The verse lists three things: the Torah, the Gospel, and "what was sent down to them from their Lord". Many commentators explain the third as the Qur'an, sent down to all people, including the People of the Book.[3]

The verse ends: "Among them is a moderate community, but many of them, evil is what they do." It is not praise of the community's handling of its scripture. It is a statement of what they would have gained if they had lived by all of God's revelation, including the last.

## Q 5:68: "you have nothing to stand on" {#5-68}

::ayah Q 5:68 | قُلْ يَـٰٓأَهْلَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ لَسْتُمْ عَلَىٰ شَىْءٍ حَتَّىٰ تُقِيمُوا۟ ٱلتَّوْرَىٰةَ وَٱلْإِنجِيلَ وَمَآ أُنزِلَ إِلَيْكُم مِّن رَّبِّكُمْ ۗ وَلَيَزِيدَنَّ كَثِيرًۭا مِّنْهُم مَّآ أُنزِلَ إِلَيْكَ مِن رَّبِّكَ طُغْيَـٰنًۭا وَكُفْرًۭا ۖ | Say: "O People of the Book! ye have no ground to stand upon unless ye stand fast by the Law, the Gospel, and all the revelation that has come to you from your Lord." It is the revelation that cometh to thee from thy Lord, that increaseth in most of them their obstinate rebellion and blasphemy.

The argument quotes the first half of this verse and stops. The second half explains the first.

"What has been sent down to you from your Lord", addressed to the Prophet, is the Qur'an, and the verse predicts that many of the People of the Book will respond to it with greater rejection. So the verse says: you have no ground until you uphold your scripture *and* the Qur'an, and many of you will refuse the Qur'an.

That is a call to the People of the Book to recognise the final revelation. It does not tell them that the books they hold are sufficient.

On the contrary, it tells them they stand on nothing until they also uphold what has come to the Prophet. Read whole, 5:68 is one of the clearest statements in the Qur'an that the earlier scriptures, as their communities held them, were not enough.

## Why God would say "uphold it" at all {#why}

The argument asks why God would tell people to uphold a book that had been altered. The answer is that the Qur'an never says the books were altered throughout.

It says they contain God's ruling {Q 5:43}, guidance and light {Q 5:44, 46}, and the description of the coming prophet {Q 7:157}, and also that parts were forgotten {Q 5:14}, concealed {Q 5:15}, and distorted {Q 5:13, 41}. Telling someone to live by the true parts of their scripture, which point to the Qur'an, is the natural thing to say on that view. Telling them to throw the book away would be the incoherent position, because it would discard the truth the Qur'an itself confirms.

## "The criterion came too late" {#too-late}

A sharper form of the objection says the Muslim reading makes the command impossible to obey. If Christians were to judge by what remained of God's revelation in their Gospel, with the Qur'an as the criterion that tells true from false {Q 5:48}, then the command came before the criterion existed. How could Christians sort their scripture before there was a Qur'an to sort it by?

The objection misplaces the verse in time. Sūrat al-Mā'idah is among the last parts of the Qur'an to be revealed, in Medina, more than twenty years into the Prophet's mission.

When 5:47 was recited, most of the Qur'an already existed, and the very next verse names it as guardian over the earlier scripture {Q 5:48}. The command and the criterion arrive in the same breath, in the same passage, addressed to the same people.

::image arcades-of-a-mosque-courtyard.webp | The arcades of a mosque courtyard | The arcades of a mosque courtyard. Photo: <a href="https://www.pexels.com/photo/minarets-over-courtyard-of-nurosmaniye-mosque-in-istanbul-18828972/">Mehmet Turgut  Kirkgoz / Pexels</a>

The objection also overlooks the Kufan reading, which makes 5:47 a statement of the purpose for which the Gospel was given to Jesus' followers, *so that* they would judge by it. On that reading there is no command to seventh-century Christians at all, and nothing that came too late.

And on the command reading, the command was never impossible. "What God revealed therein" was not hidden.

Christians of every century could judge by the commandment Jesus called the first of all, "the Lord our God, the Lord is one" {Mark 12:29}; by the law of the Torah, which the incident of the hand over the verse shows was still there to be read {Q 5:43}; and by the description of the prophet to come {Q 7:157}. Those who did so needed no criterion to see where it led.

Those who refused are the ones the verse calls *fāsiqūn*, defiantly disobedient. They turned away from what they could read.

## The same pattern in the Gospels {#gospels}

Jesus speaks to the religious teachers of his day in exactly this way. He tells them to keep the commandment of God, and in the same breath accuses them of nullifying it:

> "Thus making void the word of God by your tradition that you have handed down. And many such things you do." -- Mark 7:13 · ESV

Jesus did not tell them to abandon the Law. He told them to obey it, and accused them of having set parts of it aside in favour of human tradition.

No one concludes that he believed their teaching was free of error, or that his call to obey God's commandment certified everything they taught. He sent them back to God's word, as he understood it, against their handling of it. The Qur'an's "uphold the Torah and the Gospel" is the same kind of call.

## What the verses prove, and what they do not {#prove}

These verses prove that the Qur'an respects the revelation given to Moses and Jesus, that it regards that revelation as still present in some form among the People of the Book, and that it calls them to live by it. Muslims affirm all of that.

They do not prove that the Qur'an regarded the copies in circulation as unaltered, because the same passage says the opposite. They do not prove that the Qur'an placed those books above itself, because the same passage names the Qur'an as guardian over them and sends final judgment to it.

And they do not prove that the Qur'an's "Gospel" is the New Testament, because none of them mentions four books, apostles, letters or authors. The dilemma needs all three, and the passage supplies none.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

"Judge by it" and "uphold it" are not certificates of textual purity. They are calls to live by the revelation that remained in the People of the Book's scripture, issued in a passage that also accuses some of them of distortion and concealment and that ends by placing the Qur'an over the earlier books. Read in full, the verses lead where the passage leads: to the Qur'an.
