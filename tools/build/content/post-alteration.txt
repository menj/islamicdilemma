!lead Two claims sit near the centre of the Islamic Dilemma. The first is that "Allah, the angel Gabriel and Muhammad never said" the Gospel was corrupt, so the idea must be a later Muslim invention. The second is a set of questions: when was it corrupted, who corrupted it, and where is the uncorrupted version?

Both can be answered from the Qur'an itself, which describes the alteration of earlier scripture in more ways and more often than the argument allows.

## The claim that the Qur'an is silent {#silent}

The argument's advocates write that every reference to the Gospel in the Qur'an affirms it, and that if the Gospel had already been corrupt in the Prophet's day, the Qur'an would have said so. Their own list of verses about the Gospel is accurate as far as it goes: the verses that mention the Gospel by name speak well of it as revealed to Jesus. [Every verse on the Gospel](/every-verse-on-gospel/) reads that list in full.

But the Qur'an does not discuss the state of earlier scripture only in verses that use the word *Injīl*. It speaks about the People of the Book, about the Book given to them, and about what some of them did with it, in dozens of verses. Restricting the question to verses that happen to contain the word "Gospel" is like reading only the sentences of a report that contain a particular keyword and concluding that the report never criticises its subject.

## Six ways the Qur'an describes it {#six-ways}

The Qur'an uses at least six distinct descriptions for what happened to earlier scripture in human hands.

### 1. Distorting words from their places

The verb *ḥarrafa*, from which *taḥrīf* comes, means to tilt something, to turn it from its proper position. The Qur'an uses it repeatedly: some "distort words from their places" {Q 4:46 · 5:13}, some "distort words after they were in their places" {Q 5:41}, and a party of them "heard the word of God and then distorted it after they had understood it" {Q 2:75}. The phrase "from their places" suggests words displaced: moved, reapplied or reinterpreted so that they no longer say what they said.

### 2. Writing by hand and calling it scripture

::ayah Q 2:79 | فَوَيْلٌۭ لِّلَّذِينَ يَكْتُبُونَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبَ بِأَيْدِيهِمْ ثُمَّ يَقُولُونَ هَـٰذَا مِنْ عِندِ ٱللَّهِ لِيَشْتَرُوا۟ بِهِۦ ثَمَنًۭا قَلِيلًۭا ۖ | Then woe to those who write the Book with their own hands, and then say: "This is from Allah," to traffic with it for miserable price!

This is not a description of misinterpretation. It describes writing something and presenting it as revelation. It is the plainest statement in the Qur'an that human writing was passed off as scripture.

### 3. Twisting the tongue

::ayah Q 3:78 | وَإِنَّ مِنْهُمْ لَفَرِيقًۭا يَلْوُۥنَ أَلْسِنَتَهُم بِٱلْكِتَـٰبِ لِتَحْسَبُوهُ مِنَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ وَمَا هُوَ مِنَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ | There is among them a section who distort the Book with their tongues: (As they read) you would think it is a part of the Book, but it is no part of the Book…

This describes recitation and teaching: presenting words as scripture that are not scripture, so that hearers are misled about what the Book says.

### 4. Concealing

The Qur'an repeatedly accuses some of the People of the Book of hiding parts of their scripture. "Why do you conceal the truth while you know it?" {Q 3:71}.

"Those who conceal what God sent down of the Book" are warned {Q 2:174}. Most strikingly, it says the Prophet came "making clear to you much of what you used to conceal of the Book, and passing over much" {Q 5:15}. And in Sūrat al-Anʿām it describes the Torah being treated selectively:

::ayah Q 6:91 | تَجْعَلُونَهُۥ قَرَاطِيسَ تُبْدُونَهَا وَتُخْفُونَ كَثِيرًۭا ۖ | But ye make it into (separate) sheets for show, while ye conceal much (of its contents)…

### 5. Forgetting a portion

Of both Jews and Christians, the Qur'an says they "forgot a portion of what they were reminded of" {Q 5:13–14}. The verse about the Christians is specific: "And from those who say 'we are Christians' We took their covenant, but they forgot a portion of what they were reminded of."

Forgetting is loss, and the Qur'an keeps it apart from deliberate corruption. The Qur'an names it alongside the other forms.

### 6. Mixing truth with falsehood

"Do not mix truth with falsehood, and do not conceal the truth while you know it" {Q 2:42}; "why do you mix truth with falsehood?" {Q 3:71}. The image is of something genuine combined with something that is not, so that the two can no longer easily be told apart. That is precisely the situation in which a *muhaymin*, a guardian that can distinguish them, is needed {Q 5:48}.

So the claim that God, Gabriel and the Prophet "never said it" is simply false. The Qur'an says it in Mecca and in Medina, of Jews and of Christians, in six different ways.

## "By whom?" {#by-whom}

The Qur'an's answer to "by whom?" is careful, and it is more careful than many Muslims who repeat it. It almost never accuses the People of the Book as a whole.

It speaks of "a party of them" {Q 2:75 · 3:78}, "some of those who are Jews" {Q 4:46}, "those who write the Book with their own hands" {Q 2:79}. It also says "among the People of the Book is an upright community, who recite God's verses in the night and prostrate" {Q 3:113}, and that some of them believe in God and what was revealed to both communities {Q 3:199}.

The alteration the Qur'an describes is the work of some people, over time, not a single conspiracy by a whole religion. That is why the earlier scriptures survive in part, and why honest readers of them could recognise the Qur'an.

## "When?" {#when}

The Qur'an does not give dates, and it does not need to. It describes a process that took place across generations, before and during the Prophet's lifetime: the breaking of covenants {Q 5:13}, the forgetting of portions, the concealment it says the Prophet himself exposed {Q 5:15}. An honest account of the history of any ancient text describes the same kind of thing: changes, losses and additions accumulating over centuries of copying, translating and teaching.

That is, in fact, what the history of the New Testament's text shows. Its earliest surviving manuscripts are fragments; the earliest near-complete copies, Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, date from the fourth century.

Christian textual critics have catalogued many thousands of variants between manuscripts. Most are minor, but some are not: the longer ending of Mark {Mark 16:9–20} and the story of the woman caught in adultery {John 7:53–8:11} are absent from the earliest manuscripts, as the footnotes of modern Christian translations openly state. The early church also debated which books belonged in the New Testament at all; several, such as Hebrews, James, 2 Peter and Revelation, were disputed for generations.

None of this was hidden by Muslims. It is the conclusion of Christian scholarship. Asking "when?" as if no answer were possible ignores what Christian historians themselves have written.

## "Where is the uncorrupted version?" {#where}

This question assumes that the Muslim position requires an untouched manuscript of the original Gospel to be sitting in a library somewhere. It does not.

The Qur'an's position is that the earlier revelation survives in part inside what the People of the Book hold, and that the Qur'an itself restates and confirms its true content. The uncorrupted *message* is available: the worship of God alone, the moral law, the coming judgment, the honour of the prophets, and the description of the final prophet. It is restated in the Qur'an, which God has promised to guard {Q 15:9}.

Asking for a pristine manuscript is asking the Qur'an to have said something it never said: that the Injil survived as a separate untouched book. [Where is the Injil?](/injil-where-is-it/) answers this in detail.

## Text or interpretation? An old debate {#text-or-meaning}

Muslim scholars have long discussed whether the Qur'an's charges concern the wording of the earlier books, their interpretation, or both. A remark preserved in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* explains *yuḥarrifūna* as distorting by interpretation: no one can remove the wording of one of God's books, but they distort it by explaining it wrongly.[1]

Other scholars, such as Ibn Ḥazm, argued at length that the wording itself had been changed. Ibn Taymiyyah held a middle position: both had happened, in places, while much of the text remained.[2]

This debate is sometimes presented as a weakness, as if Muslims could not agree on their own position. It is the opposite.

It shows that the Muslim view of earlier scripture was worked out carefully, over centuries, by scholars who read the Bible's text and the Qur'an's verses closely. None of them held the position the dilemma attributes to Muslims, that the Bible is wholly preserved and authoritative.

None held its opposite, that the Bible contains nothing of God's word. All held some form of the third position the dilemma leaves out.

## The Bible says it too {#bible-says}

The Bible itself contains charges of the same kind against those who handled scripture. Jeremiah asks how the people can say "the law of the LORD is with us" when "the lying pen of the scribes has made it into a lie" {Jer 8:8}.

Jesus accuses the teachers of his day of "making void the word of God by your tradition" {Mark 7:13}. Paul warns of people who preach "a different gospel" {Gal 1:6–9}. If these are not evidence that the Bible contradicts itself, then the Qur'an's parallel charges are not evidence that it contradicts itself either.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

The Qur'an does not avoid the question of what happened to earlier scripture. It describes distortion, writing by hand, twisting the tongue, concealment, forgetting and the mixing of truth with falsehood, and it attributes them to some of the People of the Book.

It does not need to name a year, because it describes a process, one that the history of the biblical text confirms. And it does not need an untouched manuscript to exist, because it restates the message itself.
