!lead Alongside the Islamic Dilemma, its advocates publish devotional pages about identity in Christ: lists of what a Christian is, each with a verse and a short comment. Many of the comments draw contrasts with other religions. Christianity offers adoption, "unlike religions of works or distant deities".

It offers friendship, while "other religions present distant or impersonal gods". It offers boldness before God, "unlike systems of fear or rigid hierarchy".

The pages seldom name Islam, and they are written for Muslims. This post sets those contrasts beside what Islam actually teaches.

## A fair comparison {#fair}

There is nothing wrong with Christians describing what they find precious in their faith, and nothing on this page disputes that Christians experience God's love, forgiveness and nearness. The problem is with the contrasts.

A fair comparison sets the best of one tradition beside the best of another, or the teaching of one beside the teaching of the other. The devotional pages set Christian teaching, stated at its most generous, beside a description of "other religions" stated at its harshest.

Muslims reading them are told, in effect, that their God is distant, their worship is fear and their hope is a ledger of deeds. None of that is what the Qur'an says.

## "Distant deities" {#distant}

The Qur'an's God is not distant. Some of its best-known verses are about His nearness:

::ayah Q 2:186 | وَإِذَا سَأَلَكَ عِبَادِى عَنِّى فَإِنِّى قَرِيبٌ ۖ أُجِيبُ دَعْوَةَ ٱلدَّاعِ إِذَا دَعَانِ ۖ | When My servants ask thee concerning Me, I am indeed close (to them): I listen to the prayer of every suppliant when he calleth on Me…

"We are nearer to him than (his) jugular vein" {Q 50:16}. "He is with you wheresoever ye may be" {Q 57:4}. The Qur'an describes God as hearing every whisper and knowing every thought.

The Prophet conveyed God's words in a famous hadith: "I am as My servant thinks of Me, and I am with him when he remembers Me. ... If he draws near to Me a hand's span, I draw near to him an arm's length. If he comes to Me walking, I come to him running."[1] It would be hard to find a plainer picture of a God who comes toward people.

## "Impersonal gods" and friendship with God {#friendship}

The devotional pages present friendship with God as a Christian distinctive. The Qur'an speaks of it directly.

"Behold! verily on the friends of Allah there is no fear, nor shall they grieve" {Q 10:62}. It says that "Allah did take Abraham for a friend", *khalīl* {Q 4:125}.

It also speaks of love in both directions: God will bring "a people whom He will love as they will love Him" {Q 5:54}, and "If ye do love Allah, Follow me: Allah will love you and forgive you your sins" {Q 3:31}. One of God's names is *al-Wadūd*, the Loving {Q 85:14}. These are not descriptions of an impersonal god.

## "Systems where access depends on intermediaries" {#access}

One comment says that in Christ, access to God "is no longer mediated by priests or sacrifices", and that this "refutes systems where access to God depends on intermediaries or rituals". If this is meant to describe Islam, it describes the opposite of Islam.

Islam has no priesthood. No one in Islam stands between a believer and God.

Anyone may pray, at any time, in any language, and ask God directly for anything. The ritual prayer itself is led by any Muslim who knows it, with no ordained class, and a Muslim alone in a desert prays exactly as one in a great mosque.

Islam also has no sacrifice required for forgiveness. The animal sacrifice at the festival of *ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā* commemorates Abraham and is shared with the poor; the Qur'an says plainly that "It is not their meat nor their blood, that reaches Allah: it is your piety that reaches Him" {Q 22:37}. Forgiveness is asked of God directly.

## "Systems of fear" {#fear}

Another comment says God's throne "is one of grace, not terror", unlike "systems where fear of rejection dominates". Islam teaches that believers approach God with both fear and hope: they "call on their Lord, in Fear and Hope" {Q 32:16}.

Fear here means awe and a serious sense of accountability. And hope dominates: "My mercy extendeth to all things" {Q 7:156}.

In a hadith, God says: "My mercy prevails over My wrath."[2] The Prophet taught that no one should die except thinking well of God.

Christianity also teaches fear of God. Jesus said: "Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul. Rather fear him who can destroy both soul and body in hell" (Matthew 10:28).

A faith that holds fear and love together is not a "system of fear". The Qur'an and the Gospels both hold them together.

## "Religions of works" {#works}

Several comments contrast salvation "by grace through faith" with "religions of works", "systems of merit" and "endless penance". Islam is often described this way by Christian writers, but it is not how Islam describes itself.

The Prophet said that no one enters Paradise by their deeds alone, "not even me, unless God covers me with His mercy".[3] Salvation in Islam is by God's mercy, received through faith and sincere repentance. Good deeds are the fruit of faith and the means by which a believer draws near to God, and the Qur'an promises that they wipe away bad ones {Q 11:114}, but that promise is itself a gift.

There is also no "endless penance" in Islam. Repentance is turning back to God, regretting the sin and resolving not to return to it, and God accepts it: "He is the One that accepts repentance from His Servants and forgives sins" {Q 42:25}. [Sin and repentance in Islam](/sin-and-repentance/) explains this in more detail.

## "Assurance" {#assurance}

The devotional pages place heavy weight on assurance: in Christ, the believer is "free forever from condemnation", salvation is "final", nothing can separate the believer from God. Islam does not teach that any person can be certain of their final destiny while still alive, apart from those the Prophet named. It teaches hope in God's mercy together with humility about one's own heart.

But it is worth noticing that Christians themselves disagree about assurance. Some traditions teach that a true believer can never be lost.

Others teach that a believer can fall away. The same website that promises believers they are "secure" quotes, on another page, the warning that "it is impossible, in the case of those who have once been enlightened ... and then have fallen away, to restore them again to repentance" {Heb 6:4–6}.

If that passage means what the page uses it to mean, assurance is not as unconditional as the devotional lists claim. Christians can resolve that tension in their own way. They should not present one side of it as a contrast with Islam.

## "Submission only" {#submission}

One comment says that "unlike religions of submission only", Christianity calls believers into partnership with God. *Islām* does mean submission to God, and Muslims are not embarrassed by the word.

But the Qur'an also describes human beings as God's *khalīfah*, His trustee on earth {Q 2:30}, entrusted with a responsibility the heavens and mountains declined {Q 33:72}. Submission to God, in the Qur'an, is not passivity. It is the freedom of serving the One who made you rather than your desires, your fears or other people.

## The ninety-nine names {#names}

One way to see how Muslims think about God is through the names by which the Qur'an and the Prophet describe Him, traditionally counted as ninety-nine. Alongside names of power and majesty, such as the King, the Almighty and the Judge, are many names of nearness and care: the Merciful, the Especially Merciful, the Loving, the Forgiving, the Ever-Forgiving, the Accepter of Repentance, the Kind, the Gentle, the Patient, the Generous, the Near, the Responsive, the Guardian, the Friend.

Muslims call on God by these names in prayer. A religion that addresses God daily as the Loving, the Near and the Responsive is not a religion of a distant deity.

## What the contrasts leave out {#leave-out}

Each contrast follows the same pattern. A beautiful verse from the New Testament is set beside an unnamed "other religion" described in a single unfavourable phrase.

The reader is never shown a verse from the Qur'an or a saying of the Prophet. If they were, the contrast would not survive: the Qur'an's God is near, loving, merciful, a friend to His friends, approached directly without priest or sacrifice, forgiving those who turn to Him.

Muslims and Christians disagree about real things: whether Jesus is divine, whether he died for sins, what God requires for forgiveness. Those disagreements deserve careful discussion. They are not helped by describing Islam as a religion Muslims do not recognise.

Muslims, for their part, should be equally careful when they describe Christianity. A Christian reading this site should recognise their own faith in what we say about it, even where we disagree with it. That is the standard we ask of others, and the standard we try to meet.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

The God of the Qur'an is nearer than the jugular vein, answers whoever calls, loves those who turn to Him, takes friends, comes running to those who walk toward Him, and whose mercy prevails over His wrath. Islam has no priesthood, no required sacrifice for forgiveness and no endless penance. Christian devotional writing is free to celebrate what it finds in Christ, but its contrasts with "distant deities" and "systems of fear" do not describe Islam.
