!lead People rarely change religion because of a single argument, and a testimony is not a proof. What these accounts show is that the questions on this site have been taken seriously by Christians with every reason not to take them seriously: men trained for the ministry, a missionary, two priests, people with careers and reputations to lose. Each story below is summarised as the person has told it publicly, in books and talks.

Read them as accounts.

## Why testimonies matter, and their limits {#why}

Christian apologetics sites often include pages of testimonies from people who left Islam. Those accounts are real, and the people who tell them deserve to be heard with respect. Traffic between the two faiths runs in both directions, and every honest conversation should acknowledge that.

What a testimony can do is show that a question is worth asking. What it cannot do is answer it.

"One of your scholars became a Christian" invites "one of your ministers became a Muslim", and no one learns anything. The accounts below are offered for a different reason: because the people in them asked exactly the questions this site asks, about the Gospel, about Jesus and about the oneness of God, and followed the evidence to Islam.

## A Methodist minister {#minister}

**Jerald F. Dirks** grew up in a devout Methodist family in the American Midwest, studied for the ministry, earned a master's degree in divinity, and was ordained a deacon in the church. His graduate study of the history of the New Testament, he has written, left him with questions about how the Gospels were written and how the doctrine of the Trinity developed, questions his training did not answer.

Years later, through Muslim friends he met in his professional life, he began to read about Islam and found that the Qur'an's account of Jesus, a prophet and messenger who called people to worship God alone, matched what his own study of the Gospels had led him to. He became Muslim in 1993. His book *The Cross and the Crescent* compares Christian and Islamic teaching, drawing on his training as a minister, and he has written on the prophets shared by the two faiths.

**What moved him**, by his account: the convergence of two lines of evidence: the historical study of the New Testament, and the Qur'an's description of Jesus.

## A missionary who set out to find the Qur'an's mistakes {#missionary}

::portrait gary-miller.webp | Gary Miller (Abdul-Ahad Omar), Canadian mathematician and former Christian missionary, lecturing at a microphone | Gary Miller (Abdul-Ahad Omar). Public domain.

**Gary Miller** was a Canadian mathematician and a Christian missionary, active in teaching and preaching the Bible. By his own account, he took up the Qur'an with a missionary's purpose: to find the errors in it and use them to bring Muslims to Christianity. He expected a desert book of the seventh century, full of the mistakes such a book would make.

What he found, he has said, was a book that anticipated the objection he had come to make. He was struck by the challenge of Q 4:82, that a book from anyone other than God would contain much contradiction, a test no human author, aware of his own fallibility, would set, and by the Qur'an's habit of telling its reader to check it.

He also noticed what the Qur'an does not do: it does not dwell on the Prophet's own griefs, and the only woman it names, with a whole chapter called after her, is Mary, the mother of Jesus. He became Muslim in 1978, taking the name Abdul-Ahad Omar, and went on to lecture and write about the Qur'an, most widely in the talk published as *The Amazing Qur'an*.

**What moved him**, by his account: setting out to find the book's mistakes, and finding instead a book that invites exactly that search and survives it.

## An Orthodox monk and priest {#monk}

::portrait said-abdul-latif-hilarion-heagy.webp | Said Abdul Latif, formerly Father Hilarion Heagy, an Orthodox monk and priest who became Muslim | Said Abdul Latif, formerly Fr Hilarion Heagy. Public domain.

**Hilarion Heagy** was an American monk, ordained a priest in the Russian Orthodox tradition, who spent years in monastic life, latterly in an Eastern-rite monastic community in Wisconsin. His entire life was arranged around Christian prayer and the liturgy, with no career or reputation outside the church.

He had read about Islam for years and come to respect it. By his account, he accepted Islam privately in November 2022, and then concluded that he could not remain a priest and monk in public while being a Muslim in private.

He announced his conversion openly in February 2023 and took the name Said Abdul Latif. He has spoken since of finding in Islam the worship of the one God that his monastic life had been reaching for, and of the honour Islam gives to Jesus and Mary, whom he had served all his life.

**What moved him**, by his account: long study of Islam alongside a life of Christian prayer, and the conviction that God is one, which he would no longer conceal.

## A preacher who set out to convert a Muslim {#preacher}

::portrait yusuf-estes.webp | Yusuf Estes, former Christian preacher and music minister from Texas, smiling, in a black and white headscarf | Yusuf Estes. Photo: <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%D8%B5%D9%88%D8%B1%D8%A9_%D8%B4%D8%AE%D8%B5%D9%8A%D8%A9_%D9%8A%D9%88%D8%B3%D9%81_%D8%A3%D8%B3%D8%AA%D8%B3.jpg">Ayman dhū alghiná</a>, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0">CC BY-SA 4.0</a>, cropped and colour-graded.

**Yusuf Estes** was a Christian music minister and preacher in Texas, from a family active in its church, with a business in music and keyboards. By his account, he was introduced to an Egyptian Muslim through his business in the early 1990s, and he and his father set out to convert him to Christianity.

The conversations did not go as planned. Estes has described being struck by the Muslim's calm, his prayers, and his answers about God and Jesus, and by discovering that the Qur'an honours Jesus and Mary.

After months of discussion, he became Muslim in 1991, and so, he says, did members of his family and a Catholic priest who had joined their discussions. He went on to work in Muslim chaplaincy and to give talks on Islam for many years.

**What moved him**, by his account: the experience of trying to convert someone and finding that the other person's answers about God were simpler and more consistent than his own.

## A Catholic priest {#priest}

**Idris Tawfiq** was a Catholic priest in England who had studied in Rome and later became a teacher of religious education. He has described a visit to Egypt, where he saw Muslims at prayer, as the beginning of his interest in Islam. Back in Britain, his students' questions about Islam led him to read the Qur'an for himself.

He became Muslim around the turn of the millennium. He spoke and wrote widely afterwards, including for audiences of Christians, about his path to Islam, and always with respect for the church he had left.

He described his conversion as finding, in Islam, Jesus' message of worshipping one God. He died in 2016.

**What moved him**, by his account: the sight of Muslims praying, and then the Qur'an itself, read for the first time without the assumptions he had been taught about it.

## A German diplomat {#diplomat}

::portrait murad-wilfried-hofmann.webp | Murad Wilfried Hofmann, German diplomat and ambassador who became Muslim in 1980 | Murad Wilfried Hofmann. Photo: <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Murad_Wilfried_Hofmann_(cropped).JPG">Tarawneh</a>, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0">CC BY-SA 4.0</a>, cropped and colour-graded.

**Murad Wilfried Hofmann** was raised a Catholic in Germany, studied law, and spent a long career as a diplomat, rising to serve as an ambassador. He became interested in Islam while serving in North Africa, where he saw how Muslims faced war and hardship, and later through Islamic art and philosophy.

He became Muslim in 1980, and in the years that followed wrote several books explaining Islam to Western readers, including a diary of his path to Islam and *Islam: The Alternative*. He wrote with a lawyer's precision, setting out why he found the Qur'an's account of God more coherent than the doctrines he had grown up with. He died in 2020.

**What moved him**, by his account: the rationality of the Qur'an's teaching on God, which asks for belief that can be examined, and the dignity of Muslim worship.

## A mathematician {#mathematician}

::portrait jeffrey-lang.webp | Jeffrey Lang, American professor of mathematics and author of Struggling to Surrender, giving a talk | Jeffrey Lang. Public domain.

**Jeffrey Lang** was raised a Catholic in the United States, and by his own account had stopped believing in God by the time he was a teenager. He became a professor of mathematics. In his twenties he was given a copy of the Qur'an by Muslim students, and he read it, he has said, as an argument rather than as a book of devotion.

He describes the experience as being questioned by the book: it anticipated his objections and answered them. He became Muslim in the early 1980s. His books *Struggling to Surrender* and *Even Angels Ask* discuss the intellectual questions he brought with him, about God, evil and faith, and are written partly for converts and partly for Muslims raised in the faith.

**What moved him**, by his account: the Qur'an's habit of arguing with its reader, and its invitation to reflect {Q 4:82}.

## A singer {#singer}

::portrait yusuf-islam-cat-stevens.webp | Yusuf Islam, the singer formerly known as Cat Stevens, at a public gathering | Yusuf Islam. Photo: <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Yusuf_Islam_(cropped).jpg">Haroon Q Mohamoud</a>, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0">CC BY 2.0</a>, cropped and colour-graded.

**Yusuf Islam**, known to the world as the singer Cat Stevens, was raised in London in a mixed Christian household and educated in Catholic schools. He has told how, after nearly drowning off the coast of California in the mid-1970s, he promised God that if he were saved he would work for Him. Soon afterwards his brother gave him a copy of the Qur'an.

He became Muslim in 1977, and for many years left the music industry to work in education and charity. In his own account, the Qur'an's description of Jesus and of the prophets before him was what struck him first: a single message, sent through many prophets, of which he found Islam to be the completion.

**What moved him**, by his account: the Qur'an's presentation of one message from Adam to Muhammad, with Jesus honoured within it.

## What the accounts have in common {#common}

These eight people came from different churches, countries and professions. Their stories share a few things.

- They read the Qur'an themselves. None was argued into Islam by a debater. Two of them, the missionary and the preacher, set out to argue Muslims out of it. Each read the book and found it different from what he had been told.
- They found Jesus honoured. Every one of them describes discovering that Islam reveres Jesus as a prophet and the Messiah, and that becoming Muslim did not mean turning against him.
- They found the oneness of God simpler. Several describe the doctrine of the Trinity as something they had accepted rather than understood, and the Qur'an's teaching that God is one as something they could understand and examine.
- Several describe a cost. A career set aside, a reputation in their former community, strained family relationships: conversion was not free for them.

## The questions they asked {#questions}

Read side by side, the eight accounts return to the same handful of questions. Each has a full answer on this site, for readers who want to follow the same path of study.

- What did Jesus himself preach? The Gospels say he proclaimed "the gospel of God" before any Gospel was written. See [Where is the Injil?](/injil-where-is-it/)
- Did Jesus claim to be God? Read in full, the passages usually cited come with their own corrections. See [Did Jesus claim to be God?](/did-jesus-claim-to-be-god/) and [Beyond the Gospels](/beyond-gospels-do-letters-call-jesus-god/).
- Why one God, and not three? The Qur'an answers the creeds in their own words. See ["He does not beget"](/son-of-god-and-quran-5-116/).
- Can the Bible's text be trusted? The manuscripts show a stable text from the fourth century, and say nothing about the decades before. See [What the New Testament manuscripts can and cannot show](/what-new-testament-manuscripts-can-and-cannot-show/).
- What does the Qur'an say about the Gospel? It confirms what God gave Jesus and guards against what was added. See [The Islamic Dilemma, answered in full](/muslim-response/).

## Reading testimonies carefully {#carefully}

These are self-reported accounts, told years after the events, and they are summarised here in paraphrase. Details may differ between tellings, and readers who want the full story should go to the books and talks in which each person tells it. The same care applies to testimonies in the other direction.

A Muslim should also not use these stories as weapons. The Qur'an's instruction is to "argue with them in ways that are best and most gracious" {Q 16:125}, and to leave guidance to God: "thou wilt not be able to guide every one, whom thou lovest; but Allah guides those whom He will" {Q 28:56}.

A testimony can open a conversation. It cannot finish one.
