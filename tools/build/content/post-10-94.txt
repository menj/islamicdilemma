!lead Advocates of the Islamic Dilemma use Q 10:94 to argue that the Qur'an sends even the Prophet to the Bible of his day for confirmation. If the Prophet was told to settle his doubts by asking Jews and Christians, they say, their scripture must have been reliable, and the authority to settle questions lay outside the Qur'an. Read in Arabic and in its place in the surah, the verse says something different.

::ayah Q 10:94 | فَإِن كُنتَ فِى شَكٍّۢ مِّمَّآ أَنزَلْنَآ إِلَيْكَ فَسْـَٔلِ ٱلَّذِينَ يَقْرَءُونَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبَ مِن قَبْلِكَ ۚ لَقَدْ جَآءَكَ ٱلْحَقُّ مِن رَّبِّكَ فَلَا تَكُونَنَّ مِنَ ٱلْمُمْتَرِينَ | If thou wert in doubt as to what We have revealed unto thee, then ask those who have been reading the Book from before thee: the Truth hath indeed come to thee from thy Lord: so be in no wise of those in doubt.

## What the argument concedes {#concedes}

It is worth starting with what the argument's advocates themselves admit. Their own summary of the verse says that the Prophet "did not doubt", but that *if* he doubted, the resolution of the doubt was to be found outside the Qur'an, in the scriptures Jews and Christians had in their hands.

That concession matters. The verse does not describe the Prophet doubting, asking anyone, or being corrected by anyone.

It describes a hypothetical. The argument therefore rests on what the verse implies about the status of the books the People of the Book were reading. To test that implication, we need to look at the form of the verse, its subject, and the sentence that completes it.

## A conditional, addressed to the Prophet {#conditional}

The verse begins *fa-in kunta fī shakk*, "so if you are in doubt". In Arabic, as in English, a conditional sentence does not assert that its condition is true.

"If you are in doubt, ask" no more says the Prophet doubted than "if you are hungry, eat" says the listener is starving. The Qur'an uses this form elsewhere in addressing the Prophet with conditions that are plainly not meant as descriptions of him: "If thou wert to join (gods with Allah), truly fruitless will be thy work (in life)" {Q 39:65}. No reader, Muslim or not, takes that verse to mean the Prophet worshipped idols.

The classical commentators recognised the form. Many of them explain that the address is to the Prophet but the lesson is for those who heard him: anyone among his listeners who doubted could ask the People of the Book, who would confirm the account.

Al-Ṭabarī also reports, through Qatādah, that when the verse was revealed the Prophet said: "I do not doubt, and I do not ask."[1] Whatever weight one gives that report, it reflects how the earliest generations read the verse: as a rhetorical conditional, with no real doubt waiting to be resolved by another community's books.

## The sentence completes itself {#completes}

The argument usually quotes the verse up to "ask those who read the scripture before you" and stops. But the verse goes on: "The truth has come to you from your Lord, so do not be among the doubters." The next verse continues: "Nor be of those who reject the signs of Allah, or thou shalt be of those who perish" {Q 10:95}.

So the full passage says: *if* you were in doubt, the People of the Book could confirm it, but in fact the truth has come to you from your Lord, so do not doubt. The verse does not place the authority to settle the matter outside the Qur'an.

It places it in the revelation, and mentions the earlier community only as an additional witness for anyone who might hesitate. The final authority in the verse is "the truth that has come to you from your Lord".

## What the doubt would be about {#subject}

The argument also treats the verse as if it concerned the whole of the Qur'an and the whole of the Bible. Its context says otherwise.

The verses immediately before it tell the story of Moses and Pharaoh: Pharaoh's pursuit of the Children of Israel, his drowning and his last-moment declaration of faith, the preservation of his body as a sign, and God settling the Children of Israel "in a beautiful dwelling-place" {Q 10:90–93}. Verse 93 ends: "They did not differ until knowledge came to them."

The "what We have sent down to you" that the Prophet is hypothetically in doubt about is, in the first place, this account. And the People of the Book could confirm it, because the deliverance from Egypt and the drowning of Pharaoh's army are told in their own scripture, in Exodus. The verse appeals to them as witnesses to a story they knew.

Appealing to someone as a witness on a particular point is not the same as certifying everything they say. A court may call a witness to confirm that an event took place without accepting every opinion the witness holds. The Qur'an calls the People of the Book as witnesses to the story of Moses, which they did indeed read, and draws no conclusion about the integrity of every page of their scripture.

## Other verses of the same kind {#same-kind}

The Qur'an makes this kind of appeal more than once, and in each case the point is specific. When opponents objected that a prophet ought to be an angel rather than a man, it said: "And before thee also the messengers We sent were but men, to whom We granted inspiration: if ye realise this not, ask of those who possess the Message" {Q 16:43}.

The question at issue was whether earlier prophets were human. The People of the Book could confirm that they were.

Elsewhere it asks: "Is it not a Sign to them that the Learned of the Children of Israel knew it (as true)?" {Q 26:197}. And it speaks of some of the People of the Book who believe in God and in what was revealed to the Muslims and to themselves, humble before God, who do not sell His signs for a small price {Q 3:199}. The Qur'an consistently treats the honest among the People of the Book as people who could recognise its message, because it repeated and completed what their prophets had taught.

None of these verses says that the books those scholars held were free of alteration. They say that the scholars knew enough to recognise the truth. That is exactly what one would expect if the earlier scriptures survived in part, as the Qur'an says they did.

## Who "those who read the scripture" were {#who}

The commentators give names. Several mention ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām, a rabbi of Medina who accepted Islam, and others like him among the Jews and Christians who recognised the Prophet.

On this reading, the verse points to people who read their scripture honestly and found in it what confirmed the Qur'an. The witnesses are the readers of scripture, and the verse gives scripture itself no authority to overrule the Qur'an.

This also answers a question the argument does not ask. If 10:94 meant that the Bible of the seventh century was a reliable test of the Qur'an, then the People of the Book of that time, reading it, should have confirmed the Qur'an's account.

Some did, and the Qur'an praises them. Most did not, and the Qur'an explains why: some concealed what they knew {Q 2:146}, and some had lost or distorted parts of their scripture {Q 5:13}.

The verse appeals to the honest reader. It does not claim that every reader was honest or that every copy was sound.

## A parallel from the Gospels {#parallel}

The Gospel of John contains a close parallel, and it is instructive. Jesus says to his opponents:

> "You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about me." -- John 5:39 · ESV

Jesus appeals to the scriptures his opponents read as witnesses to himself. No Christian concludes from this that he endorsed every interpretation those opponents placed on their scriptures, or that their copies were beyond question.

He was saying that the scriptures, read rightly, point to him. The Qur'an makes the same kind of appeal: the earlier scriptures, read honestly, confirm the Qur'an's message. In both cases the appeal is to witness.

## What the argument would need {#would-need}

For 10:94 to support the Islamic Dilemma, the argument would need three things: that the verse describes a real doubt rather than a hypothetical; that its subject is the whole of the Qur'an measured against the whole of the Bible, rather than the story just told; and that it makes the earlier scripture the final judge, rather than calling it as a witness and then affirming that "the truth has come to you from your Lord". None of the three is in the verse.

## A note for Muslims {#for-muslims}

Muslims sometimes feel uneasy about 10:94, as if it implied that the Prophet might really have doubted. It does not, and the classical commentators never read it that way.

But the unease is worth turning into confidence. A revelation that invites its hearers to check its account against the witnesses of an earlier scripture is not afraid of comparison. That is the posture this whole site tries to take.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

Qur'an 10:94 is a conditional addressed to the Prophet, about the account of Moses and Pharaoh just given, which the People of the Book could confirm from their own scripture. It ends by declaring that the truth has come from God and telling its hearer not to doubt. It is one of several verses in which the Qur'an calls honest readers of earlier scripture as witnesses, and like the others it says nothing about the integrity of every copy those readers held.

^ Al-Ṭabarī, *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān*, on Q 10:94, reports 17893–17894 (Qatādah): «h|لا أشك ولا أسأل». The same passage names ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām among those meant by "those who read the Book before you" (from Ibn Zayd). As a report from a Successor, it is *mursal*.
