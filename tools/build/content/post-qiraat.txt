!lead When the history of the Bible comes up, a Muslim will often say that the Qur'an, unlike the Bible, exists in one form only, identical everywhere to the last dot. Christian writers have learned to answer with the qirāʾāt, the canonical readings of the Qur'an, and with the report that the caliph ʿUthmān burned rival copies. Both facts are real, and Muslims should know them better than anyone, because Muslim scholars are the ones who recorded them.

This article sets out what the readings are, what they show and what they do not, and which claims a careful Muslim should stop making.

## The objection, stated fairly {#objection}

The objection runs like this. Popular Muslim apologetics says the Qur'an has been preserved letter for letter and dot for dot, and uses that claim against the Bible, whose manuscripts differ.

But Islamic scholarship itself documents ten canonical readers and twenty transmissions, which differ in vowels, in dots, and occasionally in letters. Some of the differences change the meaning.

And the tradition reports that the third caliph standardised one codex and had the others burned. So, the objection concludes, the Qur'an has a textual history too, and Muslims apply a double standard when they fault the Bible for having one.

Part of this is factually correct, and Muslims lose nothing by saying so. The claim that the Qur'an has never been recited or written in more than one form is not true, and no classical Muslim scholar made it. The question is what the readings actually are, and whether they are the same kind of thing as the variants in the manuscripts of the New Testament.

## The readings began with the Prophet {#ahruf}

The best-known report about the readings is not a later rationalisation. It is a hadith in both *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb heard Hishām ibn Ḥakīm reciting Sūrat al-Furqān in a way he had not been taught.

He brought him to the Prophet, who asked each of them to recite. After each one, the Prophet said: "Thus it was sent down." Then he said:

::hadith إِنَّ هَذَا الْقُرْآنَ أُنْزِلَ عَلَى سَبْعَةِ أَحْرُفٍ فَاقْرَءُوا مَا تَيَسَّرَ مِنْهُ

"This Qur'an was sent down in seven *aḥruf*, so recite whatever is easy of it."[1]

Scholars have long discussed what the seven *aḥruf*, literally "letters" or "modes", were. What matters here is what the hadith establishes.

From the Prophet's own lifetime, the Qur'an was taught and recited in more than one authorised way, and the Prophet approved both recitations as revealed. Variation in recitation is therefore not evidence that the text drifted after him.

It is part of how he taught it. Aḥmad ʿAlī al-Imām, in his study of the variant readings, reaches the same conclusion from the sources: whenever the companions differed in recitation, they referred the difference to the Prophet, saying that this was how he had taught them, and the practice continued in the next generation. The ʿUthmānic orthography, he shows, was designed to preserve recitations already soundly transmitted, and the accepted readings, though they differ in wording, never contradict one another.[2]

That is the decisive difference from a scribal variant. A scribal variant arises when a copyist misreads, skips a line, or adds a clarifying phrase, and textual critics work to identify which reading is original and which is the mistake.

The canonical qirāʾāt are not mistakes awaiting correction. Each is transmitted, through named chains of teachers, as a recitation going back to the Prophet's teaching.

## One written text, more than one voicing {#rasm}

The second fact to understand is the script. Early Arabic writing recorded the consonantal outline of words, the *rasm*, often without the dots that distinguish letters of the same shape and without short vowels.

One written outline could therefore be voiced in more than one way. The canonical readings are, overwhelmingly, different voicings of the same written outline.

This can be checked against the examples usually offered as proof of variants. In the standard printed text, Q 1:4 is written with the long vowel of *Mālik*, "Owner", marked only by a small superscript sign. The written outline is the same for both readings, *Mālik*, "Owner of the Day of Judgment", and *Malik*, "King of the Day of Judgment":

::quran مَـٰلِكِ يَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ · مَلِكِ يَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ

So the claim, sometimes made, that the two readings differ by "an alif in the consonantal text" is mistaken. They share one consonantal text.

In Q 43:19, *ʿibād al-Raḥmān*, "servants of the Merciful", and *ʿinda al-Raḥmān*, "with the Merciful", differ only in the dots on one letter of the same outline. In Q 3:146, *qātala*, "fought", and *qutila*, "was killed", share one outline and differ in vowelling. In Q 2:140, *taqūlūn*, "you say", and *yaqūlūn*, "they say", differ in the dots on the first letter.

These examples show something important. The readings stay within one written text.

They are not rival texts. A reader in Morocco reciting *Malik* and a reader in Egypt reciting *Mālik* are reading the same consonants on the page.

Some readings do differ in more than dots and vowels: a letter, or occasionally a particle, present in one regional copy of the ʿUthmānic text and absent in another. The Madinan copy has *wa-awṣā* in Q 2:132 where the Iraqi copies have *wa-waṣṣā*, both meaning "and he enjoined"; the Damascus copy has *yartadid* in Q 5:54 where others have *yartadda*, two grammatical forms of "turns back"; the Makkan copy has an extra *min*, "from", in Q 9:100.

Muslim scholars recorded every such difference, copy by copy. The classical reading scholar al-Dānī explains why they exist: each was revealed and heard from the Prophet, and since one copy could not hold every authorised form, ʿUthmān distributed them among the copies he sent out.[3]

The differences are not drift. They are the authorised readings, written down deliberately.

## How the written text was fixed {#uthman}

The reports of the Qur'an's compilation are in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*. After many reciters were killed at the battle of al-Yamāmah, ʿUmar urged Abū Bakr to have the Qur'an gathered into one written collection, and Zayd ibn Thābit, one of the Prophet's scribes, was given the task.[4] Those sheets passed to ʿUmar and then to his daughter Ḥafṣah.

Under ʿUthmān, the companion Ḥudhayfah ibn al-Yamān returned from campaigns in Armenia and Azerbaijan alarmed at how differently the new Muslims of Syria and Iraq recited. "Reach this community," he told ʿUthmān, "before they differ over the Book as the Jews and Christians differed." ʿUthmān borrowed Ḥafṣah's sheets, had Zayd and three others copy them into codices, told them to follow the dialect of Quraysh where they differed, returned the sheets to Ḥafṣah, sent a copy to each region, and ordered other written copies of the Qur'an to be burned.[5]

This is the report Christian writers cite, and it is worth reading whole. The burning came after a copy had been made from the collection prepared under Abū Bakr, which was itself drawn from what the Prophet's scribes and companions had written and memorised.

Its stated purpose was to end disputes over recitation, and it was carried out within about twenty years of the Prophet's death, while many who had learned the Qur'an from him were alive to object. The tradition does not hide that some companions, notably ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, had personal codices that differed, and it records their differences. The burning removed private copies from circulation; it did not remove the knowledge that they had existed, which Muslim scholars preserved.

## How the readings were documented {#canon}

The canonical readers are not a selection made by a later authority to impose one text. They are a documentation of what was already being recited.

In the early tenth century, the Baghdad scholar Ibn Mujāhid wrote a book recording the readings of seven prominent readers of the major cities. Five centuries later, Ibn al-Jazarī recognised three more, making ten.

Ibn al-Jazarī stated the conditions for a reading to be accepted: it must agree with one of the ʿUthmānic codices, it must be sound Arabic, and it must be transmitted by a sound chain of teachers.[6] Readings outside those conditions were classed as irregular, *shādhdh*, and are not recited as Qur'an in prayer, even when they are cited for their meaning.

That is a body of scholarship sorting recitations by evidence, in public, over centuries. It is the opposite of concealment.

## The companions' personal codices {#codices}

Christian writers also point to reports that some companions' personal copies differed from the ʿUthmānic text: extra words in one, a different word order in another. The reports exist, and Muslim scholars examined them closely. Aḥmad ʿAlī al-Imām's study of the variant readings sorts them into categories and shows what they are.[7]

- Explanatory glosses. Several "additions" are a companion's own explanation written beside or into a verse in a personal copy. The words Ibn al-Zubayr is reported to have added after Q 3:110 are also attributed to ʿUthmān, which suggests they were spoken in preaching to explain the verse. ʿUthmān did not put them in the copies he sent to every region, as he would have done had he thought them Qur'an.
- Reports with weak chains. Some reported additions are contradicted by the companion's own transmitted recitation. A phrase attributed to ʿAlī after the first verse of Sūrat al-ʿAṣr does not appear in the recitation transmitted from him through his student Abū ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Sulamī, which matches the ʿUthmānic text.
- Word order. Abū Bakr is reported to have recited Q 50:19 with two words reversed. But his daughter ʿĀʾishah reported hearing him recite it, in his final illness, exactly as the standard copy has it.

The conclusion of the study is that the readings attributed to personal codices come through unsound chains, contradict one another, and are best explained as explanation, error in transmission, or recitation styles that were later set aside, whether in the Prophet's lifetime or when ʿUthmān's copies became the standard. None was left to individual choice. The Qur'an itself is what the community recited and transmitted in mass chains, and on that the companions agreed.

## What the manuscripts show {#manuscripts}

The physical record agrees with the reports.

- The Birmingham folios, two leaves in early Ḥijāzī script containing parts of Sūrahs 18 to 20, were radiocarbon-dated in 2015 to between 568 and 645, with 95 per cent probability. Their text is the standard ʿUthmānic text.
- The Tübingen manuscript, a substantial portion of the Qur'an on parchment, was radiocarbon-dated in 2014 to between 649 and 675.
- The Ṣanʿāʾ palimpsest, found in Yemen in 1972, has an upper text in the ʿUthmānic form written over an erased lower text. The lower text differs from the standard text in places, in word order, in small additions and omissions, and in phrasing. Its most detailed study argues that it is a companion codex of the kind the tradition says existed before ʿUthmān's standardisation.[8]

A careful Muslim can accept all three. The first two show the standard text in the generation of the companions. The third shows that other codices existed before it, which is exactly what Ḥudhayfah's alarm and ʿUthmān's decision presuppose.

## What Muslims should stop saying, and what they can say {#claims}

The overstatement should be retired. "Not one dot of the Qur'an has ever differed, anywhere" is not true, and a Christian who has read about the readings will know it. Overstatement weakens a true case.

The accurate claim is stronger than the slogan, because it can be checked.

- The Qur'an was written down in the Prophet's lifetime and gathered into one collection within two years of his death.
- A single consonantal text was standardised within about twenty years, from that collection, and copies were sent to every region.
- The surviving early manuscripts carry that text.
- The canonical readings are recitations of that one text, each transmitted from teacher to student back to the Prophet's teaching, recorded openly by Muslim scholars.
- None of the canonical readings changes a doctrine: God's oneness, the prophethood of Muhammad, the Qur'an's account of Jesus, and the Day of Judgment read the same in all of them.

## Is this a double standard? {#double-standard}

The Bible's manuscripts differ, and so do the Qur'an's readings. Is it inconsistent for Muslims to treat the two differently?

Not if the two are different kinds of thing. Variants in New Testament manuscripts are, by the account of Christian textual critics themselves, mostly copying changes: some accidental, some deliberate, a few large.

The ending of Mark (16:9–20) and the woman taken in adultery (John 7:53–8:11) are absent from the two oldest complete Greek Bibles, Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus. Christian scholars ask of each variant which reading the author wrote and which a later scribe introduced.

The canonical qirāʾāt are not that. They are not competing guesses at a lost original. They are recitations the tradition transmits as taught by the Prophet himself.

The honest comparison is therefore not "both have variants, so both are equal". It is that each tradition should be described as it is.

Muslims should not pretend the Qur'an has no readings. Christians should not describe authorised recitations as if they were scribal errors.

Nor does any of this touch the Islamic Dilemma itself, which is about what the Qur'an says of the Torah and the Gospel. [The Qur'an's own account of earlier scripture](/muslim-response/) does not depend on Muslims winning an argument about manuscripts.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

The qirāʾāt are a strength of the Muslim case when they are described accurately. They show a text recited in more than one authorised way from the Prophet's time, written in a single consonantal form within a generation, and documented in the open by scholars who sorted every recitation by its evidence.

What they do not support is the slogan that the Qur'an has never been recited in more than one way. Muslims lose nothing by giving up the slogan, and gain a claim that anyone can check.
