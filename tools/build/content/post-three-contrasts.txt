!lead Alongside the dilemma itself, its advocates set Bible verses beside Qur'an verses on three subjects: whether there is a mediator between God and humanity, whether God is a Trinity, and how eternal life is gained. The purpose is to show that the two books disagree so deeply that a Muslim must choose between them. The books do disagree on some of these questions, and the Qur'an says so openly.

But the contrasts as presented contain errors that should be corrected before the real disagreements can be discussed.

## Why errors matter here {#why}

We point out these errors not to score points. An argument whose whole claim is that the Qur'an misrepresents scripture must itself represent scripture accurately.

When it attaches an English verse to the wrong Arabic text, or quotes a verse about death under a heading about the Trinity, readers who cannot check the Arabic are misled about what the Qur'an says. Muslims who meet the argument should be able to see exactly where it goes wrong, and Christians who use it should want to correct it.

## Contrast one: the mediator {#mediator}

The argument quotes:

> "For there is one God and one mediator between God and mankind, the man Christ Jesus." -- 1 Timothy 2:5 · NIV

Against it, it quotes the English of {Q 4:171}: "O People of the Scripture, do not commit excess in your religion or say about Allah except the truth. The Messiah, Jesus, the son of Mary, was but a messenger of Allah ... and do not say, 'Three'; desist."

But the Arabic it prints beneath that English is not 4:171. It is a fragment of a different verse, the Throne Verse:

::ayah Q 2:255 | ٱللَّهُ لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ٱلْحَىُّ ٱلْقَيُّومُ ۚ … مَن ذَا ٱلَّذِى يَشْفَعُ عِندَهُۥٓ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِۦ ۚ | Allah! There is no god but He,—the Living, the Self-subsisting, Eternal. … Who is there can intercede in His presence except as He permitteth?

The mismatch matters, because the verse the argument actually printed undermines the contrast it was trying to draw. The Throne Verse does not deny intercession.

It says intercession happens only *by God's permission*. The Qur'an says that on the Day of Judgment intercession will benefit only those for whom God gives permission {Q 20:109}, and the hadith describe the Prophet interceding in this way.

Islam allows one person to speak on another's behalf before God. It denies that anyone shares God's authority to forgive, or that anyone must stand between a person and God for that person's prayer to be heard. "I am indeed close (to them): I listen to the prayer of every suppliant when he calleth on Me" {Q 2:186}.

The verse from 1 Timothy is also more interesting than the contrast suggests. It calls the mediator "the man Christ Jesus", distinguishing him from the "one God". A Muslim can agree that Jesus is a man and a messenger whom God sent, while disagreeing with what Christians believe he mediates.

## Contrast two: Tawhid and Trinity {#trinity}

Under the heading "Tawhid versus Trinity", the argument quotes:

> "Yet for us there is but one God, the Father, from whom all things came and for whom we live; and there is but one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom all things came and through whom we live." -- 1 Corinthians 8:6 · NIV

Against it, the English it gives is {Q 3:185}: "Every soul will taste death, and you will only be given your full compensation on the Day of Resurrection." That verse is about death and judgment; it has nothing to do with the Trinity.

It appears again, correctly, under the next heading. The Arabic printed under this heading is from {Q 4:171}, "do not say 'three'", which is the verse the heading needed. The English and Arabic have been swapped between sections.

Once the right verse is restored, the contrast is real. The Qur'an tells Christians not to say "three", and to say about God only the truth {Q 4:171}.

It says that the Messiah never disdained to be a servant of God {Q 4:172}. It says that Jesus told the Children of Israel, "worship Allah, my Lord and your Lord" {Q 5:72}. Muslims believe, and say plainly, that the doctrine of the Trinity attributes to God something He has not revealed.

But the Bible verse the argument chose is a curious one to set against the Qur'an. It says there is "one God, the Father", and separately "one Lord, Jesus Christ".

It identifies the one God as the Father. Christians read "Lord" here as a divine title, and that is their right.

Throughout Christian history, however, readers who rejected the Trinity have pointed to exactly this verse, because on its face it distinguishes the one God from Jesus. It is not obvious that it supports the side of the contrast it was chosen for.

## Contrast three: eternal life {#eternal-life}

Under "Eternal Life", the argument quotes:

> "For God so loved the world that he gave his one and only Son, that whoever believes in him shall not perish but have eternal life." -- John 3:16 · NIV

Against it, it quotes the whole of {Q 3:185}: "Every soul will taste death, and you will only be given your full compensation on the Day of Resurrection. So he who is drawn away from the Fire and admitted to Paradise has attained his desire."

The contrast implies that the Bible offers eternal life and the Qur'an offers only death. But the verse quoted says the opposite.

It says that every person dies, then is raised and judged, and that whoever is admitted to Paradise has succeeded. The Qur'an describes the people of Paradise as "their eternal home" {Q 4:57}. Islam teaches eternal life as plainly as Christianity does.

And the Bible also teaches that every person dies and is then judged:

> "And just as it is appointed for man to die once, and after that comes judgment." -- Hebrews 9:27 · ESV

So the two verses do not contrast. Both books say people die, are judged, and that the faithful receive eternal life.

The real disagreement is about the *basis* on which eternal life is given: in Christian teaching, faith in Jesus' death and resurrection; in Islam, faith in God and His messengers, sincere repentance and righteous action, received through God's mercy. That is a real difference, and [Sin and repentance in Islam](/sin-and-repentance/) discusses it. But it is not the difference the contrast presents.

## The other errors on the same page {#other-errors}

The page that presents these contrasts makes two further factual mistakes.

It says the Qur'an describes the Gospel as "a book in the hands of Christians in 700", and asks why the Prophet did not call it corrupt "if the Gospel was in their hands in 700 AD". The Prophet died in 632, and the revelation of the Qur'an ended with his death. The date is off by most of a century.

It also says that "the Bible agrees with itself 1,000 times. The Quran stands apart."

Christian scholars themselves discuss passages where the Gospels' accounts are difficult to reconcile. Mark says Jesus was crucified at "the third hour" {Mark 15:25}, while John places him before Pilate still at "about the sixth hour" {John 19:14}.

Matthew says Judas hanged himself (Matthew 27:5); Acts says he fell headlong and burst open (Acts 1:18). Christians have offered harmonisations of both, and readers can weigh them. But "agrees with itself 1,000 times" is not a description Christian scholarship would accept without qualification.

## Why accurate quotation matters to both sides {#accuracy}

Whether they come from haste or from carelessness about the Qur'an, the effect on a reader is the same. A Christian who trusts the page will believe the Qur'an says things it does not say, and a Muslim who checks it will conclude that the argument cannot be trusted on anything else.

Both outcomes are bad for honest discussion. Muslims who write about the Bible should take the same lesson: every verse quoted should be checked, in its original language, before it is used.

## The real disagreements {#real}

Once the misquotations are cleared away, real disagreements remain, and the Qur'an does not hide them. It denies that Jesus was killed or crucified: "they killed him not, nor crucified him, but so it was made to appear to them" {Q 4:157}.

It also denies that God has a son {Q 112:3} and that God is three {Q 4:171}. It affirms that Jesus was the Messiah, born of a virgin, a word from God, who worked miracles by God's permission and will return.

These disagreements do not show that the Qur'an contradicts itself. They show that it disagrees with Christian doctrine, which it says openly and repeatedly.

That is the *muhaymin* role described in {Q 5:48}: the Qur'an confirms what is true in the earlier scriptures and corrects what is not. A book that announces it will correct earlier teaching cannot be accused of inconsistency for doing so.

The dilemma needs the Qur'an to have endorsed Christian doctrine somewhere. It never does.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

Of the three contrasts the argument draws, the first prints the wrong Arabic, and the verse it does print affirms intercession by God's permission. The second gives the wrong English verse, and the Bible verse it chose distinguishes the one God, the Father, from Jesus.

The third is not a contrast: both books teach death, judgment and eternal life for the faithful. The real disagreements, about the cross and the divinity of Jesus, are ones the Qur'an states openly. They are a dispute between two books, not a contradiction within one.
