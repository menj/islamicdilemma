!lead When Muslims say the earlier scriptures did not come down unchanged, Christian apologists often answer that this is polemic: an excuse invented to escape the Bible's witness. On the Hebrew Bible, at least, the claim is not polemic. It is the settled finding of mainstream textual scholarship, much of it by scholars who hold the Bible to be scripture.

This article sets out the evidence, following section VI.A of [*Reconsidering the "Islamic Dilemma"*](https://www.academia.edu/166160379/Reconsidering_The_Islamic_Dilemma).

## The text Christians and Jews read today {#masoretic}

The Hebrew Bible in use today, in synagogues and behind almost every modern Old Testament translation, is the **Masoretic Text**. It takes its name from the Masoretes, Jewish scholars who, between about the seventh and tenth centuries CE, fixed its vowels, accents and notes. Its oldest complete manuscript, the Leningrad Codex, dates from 1008.

For a long time, scholars had few ways to see behind it. There were two other witnesses: the **Septuagint**, the Greek translation made by Jews in Egypt from the third century BCE, and the **Samaritan Pentateuch**, the five books of Moses as preserved by the Samaritans. Where these differed from the Masoretic Text, it was easy to assume the translators or the Samaritans had changed things.

Then, between 1946 and 1956, the caves near Qumran gave up the **Dead Sea Scrolls**, copied between about 250 BCE and 68 CE. They include fragments of every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther, a thousand years older than the Leningrad Codex. And they showed that the picture was wrong.

## Several texts, side by side {#several}

Emanuel Tov, the leading authority on the textual criticism of the Hebrew Bible, classifies the biblical scrolls from Qumran into five groups:

- texts close to the later Masoretic Text;
- texts close to the Samaritan Pentateuch, without its specifically Samaritan changes;
- texts close to the Hebrew that the Septuagint translators used;
- texts that match none of these;
- texts copied in a distinctive Qumran scribal style.

His conclusion is that scholars should no longer put the Masoretic Text at the centre of their thinking about the text.[1] Before 70 CE, the Hebrew Bible existed in several forms at once, and Jewish communities used them side by side.

The Masoretic Text is one of those forms, the one that rabbinic Judaism later standardised. It is not the single original from which the others strayed.

## What the scrolls show, book by book {#examples}

**Isaiah.** The Great Isaiah Scroll from Cave 1, the best-known scroll of all, is complete. It differs from the Masoretic Text at well over 2,600 points.

Most are small matters of spelling, but not all. In the Servant Song, at Isaiah 53:11, the scroll reads that after his suffering the servant "shall see light".

The Septuagint has the same word, and the Masoretic Text lacks it. Several modern translations, including the NRSV and the NIV, now include "light"; the ESV gives it in a footnote.[2]

**Samuel.** The Samuel scroll known as 4QSamᵃ agrees with the Greek Septuagint against the Masoretic Text in 183 documented places.[3] It also preserves a whole paragraph the Masoretic Text has lost: an account of the Ammonite king Nahash gouging out the right eyes of the men of Gad and Reuben, which the Jewish historian Josephus also knew. The NRSV restores it, at the end of 1 Samuel 10.

**Jeremiah.** The Septuagint's Jeremiah is about one-eighth shorter than the Masoretic book, and arranges the oracles against the nations differently. Fragments from Qumran, 4QJerᵇ and 4QJerᵈ, show that the shorter form existed in Hebrew. Tov concludes that the two are successive editions of the book, and that both were treated as authoritative at Qumran.

**Job.** The Septuagint's Job is about one-sixth shorter than the Hebrew.

**Esther.** The Greek Esther contains six major additions, 107 verses, that the Masoretic Text does not have. Notably, the Hebrew Esther never mentions God at all; the Greek additions do.

**Daniel.** The Greek Daniel adds three passages: the story of Susanna, the story of Bel and the Dragon, and the Prayer of Azariah with the song of the three young men in the furnace.

**Genesis.** The ages of the patriarchs in Genesis 5 and 11, and so the total span from Adam to Abraham, differ between the Masoretic Text, the Septuagint and the Samaritan Pentateuch. The difference in the total chronology runs from 600 to 1,400 years.[4]

## A disputed mountain {#gerizim}

The Samaritan Pentateuch differs from the Masoretic Text at thousands of points. Scholars now separate two layers in it: a large body of older readings, many of which also appear in Dead Sea Scrolls, and a smaller set of specifically Samaritan changes.

The most charged difference is in Deuteronomy 27:4, where Moses commands an altar to be built after Israel enters the land. The Masoretic Text says it is to be built on **Mount Ebal**. The Samaritan Pentateuch says **Mount Gerizim**, the Samaritans' holy mountain.

For a long time, "Gerizim" was assumed to be a Samaritan alteration. But it is also the reading of a small Qumran fragment of Deuteronomy, of the Old Latin version, and of a Greek papyrus from the fifth or sixth century. Several textual critics now conclude that "Gerizim" was the original, and that "Ebal" is the later change, made in the Jewish text.[5]

If they are right, a verse about the place of God's altar was altered in the very text that underlies modern Bibles, and for a reason that had to do with a dispute between two communities.

## The evidence at a glance {#table}

| Witness or book | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Great Isaiah Scroll | Over 2,600 differences from the Masoretic Text. "Light" at Isaiah 53:11, with the Septuagint. |
| 4QSamᵃ | 183 agreements with the Septuagint against the Masoretic Text, and a lost paragraph about Nahash. |
| Jeremiah | The Septuagint is about one-eighth shorter; two editions of the book, both in Hebrew. |
| Job | The Septuagint is about one-sixth shorter. |
| Esther | Six Greek additions, 107 verses, absent from the Masoretic Text. |
| Daniel | Susanna, Bel and the Dragon, and the Prayer of Azariah in the Greek. |
| Genesis | Patriarchal chronologies differ by 600 to 1,400 years between the three traditions. |
| Deuteronomy 27:4 | Ebal in the Masoretic Text; Gerizim in the Samaritan, with Qumran, Old Latin and Greek support. |

## What this does and does not show {#does-not}

None of this means the Hebrew Bible is a forgery, or that its text is unknowable. Most of the differences are small, and textual critics can often explain how they arose. A Muslim gains nothing by exaggerating the evidence, and the Qur'an does not ask anyone to.

Nor is the idea of a changed text a Muslim import. The rabbinic tradition itself preserves a list of *tiqqunei soferim*, "corrections of the scribes": places, eighteen in the best-known list, where it records that the scribes altered the wording of the text out of reverence, to avoid an expression that seemed to speak of God improperly.

The Masoretes also marked words to be read differently from the way they were written, the *qere* and *ketiv*. Jewish scholarship knew and recorded that its copyists had made choices.

What the evidence does show is that the text was not fixed in the centuries before and around the time of Jesus. Different communities had different forms of the same books, some longer, some shorter, some with passages the others lacked.

The form that became standard was chosen, by a community, from among several. That is a history of human transmission, with human choices in it.

## Why this matters for the dilemma {#dilemma}

The dilemma assumes that when the Qur'an affirms the Torah, it affirms a fixed text identical to the Hebrew Bible in modern hands. The scrolls make that assumption impossible.

In the centuries before the Qur'an, there was no single fixed Hebrew text for anyone to affirm. There were editions.

This is exactly the situation the Qur'an describes. It says the Torah was *entrusted* to the rabbis and scholars, who "were witnesses thereto" {Q 5:44}.

Custody by people is something that can be done well or badly, and leaves the kind of record the scrolls show. The Qur'an makes a different promise about itself: "We have, without doubt, sent down the Message; and We will assuredly guard it (from corruption)" {Q 15:9}. The difference between the two regimes is set out in [Does the Qur'an's preservation depend on "none can change His words"?](/does-quran-15-9-depend-on-6-115/)

The Qur'an affirms that God revealed the Torah to Moses. It does not affirm that any later edition is that revelation, word for word. The Dead Sea Scrolls are what one would expect if the Qur'an is right: a revelation honoured and copied, and a text that, in human hands, went through more than one form.
