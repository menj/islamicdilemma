!lead Some answers to the Islamic Dilemma avoid both of its claims by proposing a third option. The Qur'an, they say, affirms the Torah and the Gospel and then gives their true meaning, just as the New Testament affirms the Old Testament and reads it in a new light. The proposal contains something right and something the verses do not support, and the two are worth separating.

## What the proposal says {#proposal}

The Islamic Dilemma offers two choices. Either the Gospel is reliable, so the Qur'an contradicts it, or the Gospel is corrupt, so the Qur'an wrongly told Christians to judge by it.

The third option says that the choice is false. A later revelation can honour an earlier one and still disclose a meaning the earlier community missed. Scholars of Jewish and Christian interpretation sometimes call this "revelatory exegesis", a name taken from the study of those traditions and not from Islamic scholarship.

The proposal adds a comparison. The New Testament affirms the Old Testament strongly and also differs from it on the nature of God, on the law and on atonement. If Christians may hold both positions, the argument goes, so may Muslims.

## What is sound in it {#sound}

The proposal is right that the Qur'an does not tell Muslims to discard the earlier scriptures. It says the Qur'an came "confirming what went before it", and it names the Law and the Gospel as sent before as "a guide to mankind" {Q 3:3}.

It is also right that the Qur'an does not ask the reader to treat the Bible of the seventh century, or of any century, as the final judge. Q 5:48 calls the Qur'an a guardian over the earlier scripture. That is a claim to authority over its meaning, and the claim is made in the verse itself.

Most of the classical scholars agree with a large part of this. They placed the corruption in meaning, and read the Bible through the Qur'an as criterion. The article on [what the classical scholars said about *taḥrīf*](/what-classical-muslim-scholars-said-about-tahrif/) sets out the evidence.

## Where the verses say more {#more}

The proposal asks a few words to carry more than they do. Three points need correcting.

### "Confirm" does not mean "fulfil"

The proposal reads *muṣaddiq*, "confirming", as "fulfilling", on the ground that Jesus in Q 3:50 confirms the Torah and also makes lawful what was forbidden. The verse separates the two acts.

Jesus says he comes "to attest the Law which was before me. And to make lawful to you part of what was (Before) forbidden to you" {Q 3:50}.

In the Arabic the two are coordinate: *wa-muṣaddiqan li-mā bayna yadayya mina al-tawrāh wa-li-uḥilla lakum baʿḍa alladhī ḥurrima ʿalaykum*. The second clause has its own purpose, introduced by its own particle. If making lawful were part of the meaning of *muṣaddiq*, the verse would not need to state it.

The same word is used of Jesus in Q 61:6, "confirming the Law (which came) before me". It is used of the Qur'an in Q 46:12, "this Book confirms (it)", and again in Q 46:30. In these verses what is confirmed is the Torah, the Book of Moses or what came before, and none of them speaks of fulfilling it.

One objection is that the Qur'an never says it confirms the Gospel by name. That is true of the verbs in these verses.

But Q 3:3 says it confirms "what went before it" and lists the Law and the Gospel among what came before, and Q 5:48 says it confirms "the scripture that came before it". The confirmation covers the earlier revelation as a whole.

### A text left intact cannot explain the charge

The proposal needs the earlier texts to be broadly intact and misread. The Qur'an says more than that about what some of the People of the Book did.

It says some "change the words from their (right) places" {Q 5:13}. It says a party of them "heard the Word of God, and perverted it knowingly after they understood it" {Q 2:75}. And it describes a section who "distort the Book with their tongues: (As they read) you would think it is a part of the Book, but it is no part of the Book; and they say, 'That is from God,' but it is not from God" {Q 3:78}.

The last verse speaks of words that sound like the Book and are not part of it. A reading that finds only a different interpretation of the same text has no place for that. The same holds for Q 2:79, which speaks of those who "write the Book with their own hands, and then say: 'This is from God'".

The Qur'an does not say how much was lost or added, and the classical scholars differed on whether the change was chiefly in the wording or in the meaning. The point here is narrower. The Qur'an's own charge includes the passing off of human words as God's, and an account that leaves the text untouched cannot account for it.

Al-Fārūqī reaches the same position from a different direction. For him the earlier religions are to be faulted in the historical form of their texts, and the revelations they received remain honoured.[1]

### A comparison does not settle the question

The last step is the argument that Christians do the same, so Muslims may. The comparison is worth examining on its merits, and the answer is that it settles nothing about what the Qur'an says.

Whether the New Testament reads the Old Testament faithfully is a question for Christians and for historians of the text. It does not decide what Q 3:81, Q 5:48 or Q 10:94 mean. The dilemma is posed about the Qur'an's own words, and those words are read for themselves.

A comparison can show that holding two positions at once is possible. It cannot show that the Qur'an holds them.

## Older precedents for rereading {#precedents}

The method itself is older than the New Testament. Jeremiah gives Babylon a term: "these nations shall serve the king of Babylon seventy years" {Jer 25:11}.

In Daniel 9, Daniel reads "in the books" the number of years given to Jeremiah, seventy {Dan 9:2}. Then the angel Gabriel comes to him, and the figure becomes "seventy weeks" {Dan 9:24}. The later book keeps the earlier number and gives it a new sense, on the authority of a revelation.

The Commentary on Habakkuk from Qumran Cave 1 (1QpHab) does the same on purpose. In column 7 it says that God told Habakkuk to write what would come on the last generation and did not make known to him when that time would end. To the Teacher of Righteousness, it says, God made known all the mysteries of the words of His servants the prophets.[2]

So a later authority saying what an earlier text means is a practice Jews followed before Christianity and before Islam.

It shows the method is possible. It does not show the Qur'an uses it, which is why the verses above are read for themselves. The examples are worked through in [how scripture rereads scripture, from Daniel to the Qur'an](https://bestofislam.org/revelatory-exegesis/).

## Where rereading stops {#seal}

The obvious reply is a question. If the Qur'an may reread the Torah and the Gospel, why may Joseph Smith not reread the Qur'an, or Bahā'u'llāh?

The Qur'an answers it in its own words. Muhammad is "the Messenger of God, and the Seal of the Prophets" {Q 33:40}. A rereading by revelation needs a prophet to receive the revelation, and after the seal there is none.

So the Qur'an's claim to judge the earlier record is made once, by the last prophet, and it closes the line.

A later claimant who rereads the Qur'an has first to set aside 33:40, and that is a quarrel with the Qur'an itself. The Qur'an's own claim sits inside it.

The commentators and hadith on the verse are gathered in [can a later prophet reinterpret the Qur'an?](https://bestofislam.org/seal-of-the-prophets/)

## What a good version of the third option says {#good}

A sound form of the proposal would say three things. The Qur'an confirms the revelation God gave through the earlier prophets.

It judges the later record of that revelation, as the guardian of it. And it says that some of those who held the record changed its words and passed their own words off as God's.

That version avoids both claims of the dilemma, and it is the position this site takes throughout. It does not need "fulfil" for *muṣaddiq*, and it does not need the earlier texts to be untouched.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

The third option is right that the Qur'an affirms the earlier revelation and claims authority over how it is read. It goes too far when it turns "confirm" into "fulfil", leaves the text untouched against the Qur'an's own charge, and treats a comparison with the New Testament as an answer.
