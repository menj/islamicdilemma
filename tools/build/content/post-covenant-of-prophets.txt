!lead Advocates of the Islamic Dilemma point to Q 3:81 as the verse that makes the earlier scriptures the test of the Prophet. God took a covenant from the prophets that a messenger would come "confirming what is with you", so a messenger whose message differs from the Bible, they say, has failed the test God set. The verse deserves a careful reading, because it says less about a test and more about a promise.

::ayah Q 3:81 | وَإِذْ أَخَذَ ٱللَّهُ مِيثَٰقَ ٱلنَّبِيِّۦنَ لَمَآ ءَاتَيْتُكُم مِّن كِتَٰبٍۢ وَحِكْمَةٍۢ ثُمَّ جَآءَكُمْ رَسُولٌۭ مُّصَدِّقٌۭ لِّمَا مَعَكُمْ لَتُؤْمِنُنَّ بِهِۦ وَلَتَنصُرُنَّهُۥ ۚ قَالَ ءَأَقْرَرْتُمْ وَأَخَذْتُمْ عَلَىٰ ذَٰلِكُمْ إِصْرِى ۖ قَالُوٓا۟ أَقْرَرْنَا ۚ قَالَ فَٱشْهَدُوا۟ وَأَنَا۠ مَعَكُم مِّنَ ٱلشَّٰهِدِينَ | Behold! God took the covenant of the prophets, saying: "I give you a Book and Wisdom; then comes to you a messenger, confirming what is with you; do ye believe in him and render him help." God said: "Do ye agree, and take this my Covenant as binding on you?" They said: "We agree." He said: "Then bear witness, and I am with you among the witnesses."

## What the argument says {#argument}

The argument runs in three steps. God took a covenant from the prophets, and the covenant says the coming messenger will confirm what they hold.

The Qur'an, it concludes, contradicts the Bible on the nature of Jesus and on the cross, so Muhammad is not the messenger the covenant describes.

The argument has real force at one point, and it is better to say so. The verse does make confirmation a mark of the messenger. A reader who takes "confirming what is with you" to mean "agreeing with every sentence in the Bible now in circulation" will find the conclusion hard to avoid.

The question is whether the verse says that.

## Who is bound {#bound}

The covenant is taken from the prophets, and the obligation falls on them. They are told to "believe in him and render him help", and they answer: "We agree." The verse sets out no examination of the messenger and no committee of scripture to rule on him.

The commentators read it this way. Al-Ṭabarī reports from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib that God sent no prophet, from Adam onward, without taking his covenant concerning Muhammad: that if he were sent in the prophet's lifetime, the prophet would believe in him and help him, and would take the same covenant from his people.[1]

Al-Ṭabarī reports a similar account from Ibn ʿAbbās. The chain for the report from ʿAlī includes Sayf ibn ʿUmar, whom the critics of hadith weaken, so it is better to rest the point on the view al-Ṭabarī himself prefers.

That view, al-Ṭabarī explains, is that the covenant is one the prophets made to confirm one another, and that each prophet took the same covenant from his people. His reason is plain: no prophet was sent to a community with an order to deny another of God's prophets.

The same idea appears elsewhere in the Qur'an. "And remember We took from the prophets their covenant: As (We did) from thee: from Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus the son of Mary: We took from them a solemn covenant" {Q 33:7}. The covenant of the prophets concerns their mission and the way they stand toward one another.

## "What is with you" {#with-you}

The phrase *li-mā maʿakum* means "what is with you". In the verse, those addressed are the prophets, and what is with them is the revelation each received: "a Book and Wisdom".

The grammarians record two readings of the opening of the clause, *lamā* and *limā*, and analyse the construction differently. Neither changes who made the covenant or who is bound by it.[2]

A reader might object that the Qur'an uses the same phrase of the Jews of Medina. It tells them to believe in what it reveals, "confirming the revelation which is with you" {Q 2:41}, and says they reject "Truth confirming what is with them" {Q 2:91}.

That is a fair point, and the same passage answers it. The verse after Q 2:41 says: "And cover not Truth with falsehood, nor conceal the Truth when ye know (what it is)" {Q 2:42}. Later in the surah it speaks of those who "write the Book with their own hands, and then say: 'This is from God'" {Q 2:79}.

So "what is with you" names the truth that God gave through their prophets, and the Qur'an says in the same passage that some of them covered it with falsehood. The phrase does not certify every page that a later community kept.

## What "confirming" means {#confirming}

The word is *muṣaddiq*, from the root *ṣ-d-q*, truthfulness. To confirm in this sense is to attest that a message is true.

The Qur'an uses the word of itself in Q 5:48, "confirming the scripture that came before it, and guarding it in safety". The same verse says the Qur'an is *muhaymin* over the earlier scripture, a guardian and a witness. The article on [*muṣaddiq* and *muhaymin*](/what-does-quran-5-48-confirm/) looks at the two words together.

It also uses the word of Jesus, "confirming the Law that had come before him" {Q 5:46}, and the Jesus of Q 3:50 says he comes "to attest the Law which was before me" and "to make lawful to you part of what was (Before) forbidden to you". A messenger who attests the Law and also changes a detail of it is not copying the earlier text sentence by sentence.

What the Qur'an confirms is the message that every prophet brought: that God is one, that He alone is worshipped, and that every person will answer for his deeds. Al-Fārūqī calls these the core of al-tawḥīd and says all the prophets taught them.[3]

## The consequence the verse names {#consequence}

The next verse states what follows from the covenant: "If any turn back after this, they are perverted transgressors" {Q 3:82}. The consequence falls on those who turn back from the covenant they made, and the covenant is to believe in the messenger and to help him.

That is the opposite of the argument's order. The argument asks the messenger to pass an examination first. The verse commits the prophets, and through them their communities, to accept him when he comes.

The verse that follows shows the same attitude. "We make no distinction between one and another among them" {Q 3:84}. The believer is told to accept every prophet and every revelation that came from God.

## Found in their own scriptures {#found}

A related verse describes the Prophet as one "whom they find mentioned in their own (scriptures),- in the law and the Gospel" {Q 7:157}. That is a claim about what honest readers of earlier scripture would recognise, and the covenant of Q 3:81 is its other side.

The Qur'an expects the prophets and their followers to recognise him. It also says that many did not, and gives reasons: they concealed what they knew {Q 2:42}, and they forgot a good part of the message {Q 5:13}.

## What the argument would need {#would-need}

For Q 3:81 to support the Islamic Dilemma, the argument would need three things. It would need the verse to make the Bible now in circulation the examiner of the messenger, when the verse addresses the prophets.

It would need "confirming" to mean agreement with every sentence, when the Qur'an uses the word for attesting that a message is true. And it would need "what is with you" to certify later copies, when the Qur'an says in the same passage that some covered the truth with falsehood.

None of the three is in the verse.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

The covenant of the prophets is a promise made by the prophets to believe in the messenger who confirms what God gave them, and to help him. The classical commentators read it as a covenant among the prophets, each taking the same covenant from his people.

The Qur'an confirms the revelation the prophets received. It also says what later hands did to it. Read with the verses around it, Q 3:81 supports both statements, and it supplies no test that the Qur'an fails.
