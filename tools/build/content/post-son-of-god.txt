!lead A favourite Christian claim about Islam is that the disagreement over Jesus rests on a misunderstanding. The Qur'an, it says, objects to "Son of God" because it imagines God taking a wife and fathering a child, and Christians reject that idea as firmly as Muslims do. It adds that Q 5:116 seems to think the Trinity includes Mary, which no Christian creed has ever taught.

If both are true, the Qur'an is rejecting a caricature. This article looks at what the Qur'an actually says about sonship and about Mary, in Arabic and in context.

## The objection, stated fairly {#objection}

The objection has two parts. First, Q 6:101 asks how God could have a son "when He has no consort", and Q 112:3 says God "does not beget".

Christians, it says, have never believed God had a consort or begot a son physically. In the New Testament "Son" means sharing the Father's nature in an eternal relationship, and the virgin conception is the work of the Holy Spirit, not a union.

Second, Q 5:116 has God ask Jesus whether he told people to take him and his mother as two gods besides God. Since no Christian confession counts Mary as a person of the Trinity, the verse is taken to describe a Christianity that did not exist.

Both parts misread the Qur'an.

## The Qur'an rejects more than physical fatherhood {#ittakhadha}

Q 6:101 does mention a consort, and some Arabs of the Prophet's time did speak of God having daughters, as the Qur'an reports elsewhere {Q 16:57}. But the Qur'an's rejection of sonship is not limited to that verse, and its central verb is not about fathering at all.

::ayah Q 19:88 | وَقَالُوا۟ ٱتَّخَذَ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنُ وَلَدًۭا | They say: "(Allah) Most Gracious has begotten a son!"

::ayah Q 19:92 | وَمَا يَنۢبَغِى لِلرَّحْمَـٰنِ أَن يَتَّخِذَ وَلَدًا | For it is not consonant with the majesty of (Allah) Most Gracious that He should beget a son.

The verb is *ittakhadha*, "to take", "to adopt", "to take for oneself". It is the verb the Qur'an uses for taking a friend, a guardian or an ally.

A son one *takes* is not necessarily a son one fathers. The Qur'an denies that God takes a son in any way at all, physical or otherwise, and says such a thing is not fitting for Him. The same verb appears in the Qur'an's answer to the claim in Sūrat al-Baqarah:

::ayah Q 2:116 | وَقَالُوا۟ ٱتَّخَذَ ٱللَّهُ وَلَدًۭا ۗ سُبْحَـٰنَهُۥ ۖ بَل لَّهُۥ مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۖ كُلٌّۭ لَّهُۥ قَـٰنِتُونَ | They say: "Allah hath begotten a son": Glory be to Him.—Nay, to Him belongs all that is in the heavens and on earth: everything renders worship to Him.

The answer here is not "God has no wife". It is that everything in creation belongs to God and obeys Him.

The relationship between God and every creature, Jesus included, is that of Owner and owned, Lord and servant. Nothing in creation shares His nature. That is an objection to sonship in exactly the sense the objection says Christians mean it: a son "sharing the Father's nature".

## The creeds use the language of begetting {#creed}

The second Qur'anic text, "He begetteth not, nor is He begotten" {Q 112:3}, is sometimes said to reject only physical procreation. But the language of begetting is the Christian creeds' own. The Nicene Creed, in the form used by most churches, confesses Jesus as

> "the only-begotten Son of God, begotten of his Father before all worlds, God of God, Light of Light, very God of very God, begotten, not made, being of one substance with the Father" -- The Nicene Creed (381), Book of Common Prayer translation

The creed does not mean physical begetting. It means that the Son is eternally from the Father and shares His substance.

That is precisely what Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ denies. "He does not beget" denies that anything comes forth from God sharing His nature; "nor was He begotten" denies that anything is the source of God.

The Qur'an and the creed are using the same word and disagreeing about it directly. Neither is misunderstanding the other.

## The Qur'an also says what Jesus is instead {#instead}

The Qur'an also states what Jesus is: "He was no more than a servant: We granted Our favour to him, and We made him an example to the Children of Israel" {Q 43:59}, "Christ Jesus the son of Mary was (no more than) a messenger of Allah, and His Word, which He bestowed on Mary, and a spirit proceeding from Him" {Q 4:171}.

The same verse says: "God is only one God. Glory be to Him above having a son." Its comparison is with Adam: created by God's command, "Be", without a father {Q 3:59}.

This is not a misunderstanding of Christian teaching. It is a deliberate alternative to it.

The Qur'an knows that Christians call Jesus "the son of Allah" {Q 9:30}, and quotes them saying so accurately. It knows that some say "Allah is one of three in a Trinity" {Q 5:73}, and others that "Allah is Christ the son of Mary" {Q 5:72}. It answers each claim in its own terms.

## Q 5:116: what the verse actually says {#5-116}

::ayah Q 5:116 | وَإِذْ قَالَ ٱللَّهُ يَـٰعِيسَى ٱبْنَ مَرْيَمَ ءَأَنتَ قُلْتَ لِلنَّاسِ ٱتَّخِذُونِى وَأُمِّىَ إِلَـٰهَيْنِ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ ۖ | And behold! Allah will say: "O Jesus the son of Mary! Didst thou say unto men, 'worship me and my mother as gods in derogation of Allah'?"

Three things should be noticed.

1. The verse does not mention the Trinity. It says nothing about three persons or one substance. The phrase "the third of three" occurs in a different verse {Q 5:73}, which also does not mention Mary. The claim that the Qur'an thinks the Trinity is Father, Son and Mary is built by joining two separate verses that the Qur'an does not join.
2. The verse is about worship. *Ittakhidhūnī wa-ummī ilāhayn* means "take me and my mother as two gods", that is, as objects of worship. The question is not what a creed says about the being of God. It is what people did: whom they prayed to and venerated.
3. **The verb is again *ittakhadha*.** People "take" Jesus and Mary as gods. The verse describes human practice, not a doctrine Christians signed.

Read that way, the verse describes something real. By the seventh century, devotion to Mary was a central part of Christian worship across the Byzantine world.

The Council of Ephesus in 431 had given her the title *Theotokos*, "God-bearer" or "Mother of God". Churches were dedicated to her, hymns addressed her, and Christians asked for her intercession.

The fourth-century writer Epiphanius condemned a group he called the Collyridians, who offered cakes to Mary in worship. Christian theology distinguishes the veneration of Mary from the worship due to God.

But a verse about what people *took* as objects of devotion does not need a creed to make Mary divine. It needs only the practice, and the practice existed.

## What Jesus answers {#answer}

The verse continues with Jesus' reply, and the reply is the point of the passage:

::ayah Q 5:117 | مَا قُلْتُ لَهُمْ إِلَّا مَآ أَمَرْتَنِى بِهِۦٓ أَنِ ٱعْبُدُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ رَبِّى وَرَبَّكُمْ ۚ | "Never said I to them aught except what Thou didst command me to say, to wit, 'worship Allah, my Lord and your Lord'…

The scene clears Jesus of what was done in his name. It is not a statement about the Nicene Creed.

It is Jesus disowning the worship given to him and to his mother, and restating the message the Gospels also record: "I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God" {John 20:17}. Al-Ṭabarī records two views on when God asks the question: at the moment He raised Jesus to Himself, after Christians had begun to say what they said about him, or on the Day of Judgment.[1] On either view, the question is put to Jesus because of what others claimed he had taught.

## Why the "misunderstanding" argument fails {#fails}

The argument that the Qur'an rejects a caricature depends on reading two verses, 6:101 and 5:116, in isolation and in the narrowest sense. Read with the rest of the Qur'an, the picture is different.

- The Qur'an denies that God *takes* a son in any sense {Q 19:88–92 · 2:116}, which includes fathering one.
- It denies begetting in the creeds' own vocabulary {Q 112:3}.
- It reports Christian statements accurately {Q 9:30 · 5:72–73}.
- Its question about Mary concerns worship and veneration, which were real, not a creed that never existed {Q 5:116}.

Christians and Muslims do disagree about who Jesus is. That disagreement is not a confusion to be cleared up by explaining that "Son" is a relational term. The Qur'an understood the claim and rejected it: God is one, nothing shares His nature, and Jesus was His servant and messenger.

## The Qur'an needed no caricature {#no-caricature}

The "misunderstanding" argument assumes the Qur'an had to distort Christian belief to reject it. It did not. It quotes what Christians say, "Christ the son of Allah" {Q 9:30}, "Allah is Christ the son of Mary" {Q 5:72}, "Allah is one of three in a Trinity" {Q 5:73}, and rejects each statement as it stands.

Put Christian belief in its own most careful terms and the Qur'an's answer does not move. Christians confess that Jesus is the eternal Son of God, begotten of the Father and of one substance with Him, and that Mary is to be honoured above every saint.

The Qur'an denies that God takes a son in any sense, denies that anything is begotten of Him, and warns against taking Jesus and his mother as objects of worship. Explaining that "Son" is meant relationally changes nothing. The relationship itself, a person who shares God's nature, is what the Qur'an calls *shirk*: associating a partner with God, the one sin it says God will not forgive {Q 4:48}.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

The claim that the Qur'an misunderstood "Son of God" does not survive a reading of the Qur'an. The Qur'an's denial covers taking a son as well as fathering one, meets the creeds' language of begetting directly, and describes the veneration of Mary as a practice rather than a doctrine. The disagreement is real, and it is about the oneness of God.

^ Al-Ṭabarī, *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān*, on Q 5:116, reports 13028 (al-Suddī) and 13029 (Ibn Jurayj).
