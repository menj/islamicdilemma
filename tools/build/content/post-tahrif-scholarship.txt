!lead The Islamic Dilemma needs Islam to hold a simple position: that the Torah and the Gospel were rewritten from end to end, so that a Muslim who quotes them contradicts himself. That position is easy to refute, which is why the argument assigns it to Muslims. The classical record shows something else. This article sets out what the major scholars actually said, drawing on the survey in [*Reconsidering the "Islamic Dilemma"*](https://www.academia.edu/166160379/Reconsidering_The_Islamic_Dilemma), the study on which this site is built.

## The word, and where the Qur'an uses it {#word}

*Taḥrīf* means "distortion" or "turning aside". As a noun, the word does not occur in the Qur'an at all. The verb *yuḥarrifūna*, "they distort", occurs four times, at Q 2:75, 4:46, 5:13 and 5:41, and each time it describes certain Jews of the Prophet's time. The Qur'an never uses the verb of Christians.

When the Qur'an turns to the Christians, it uses a different word:

::ayah Q 5:14 | وَمِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا نَصَـٰرَىٰٓ أَخَذْنَا مِيثَـٰقَهُمْ فَنَسُوا۟ حَظًّۭا مِّمَّا ذُكِّرُوا۟ بِهِۦ | And from those who say, "We are Christians", We took their covenant, but they forgot a portion of what they were reminded of.

They *forgot*. The charge against the Christians is loss and neglect, not forgery. A Christian apologist who builds the dilemma on the claim that the Qur'an accuses the New Testament of textual forgery is answering a charge the Qur'an does not make.

## Two kinds of distortion {#two-kinds}

The classical scholars distinguished two kinds of *taḥrīf*:

- ***Taḥrīf al-lafẓ***, distortion of the wording: changing, adding or removing words in the written text.
- ***Taḥrīf al-maʿnā***, distortion of the meaning: reading a text against its sense, explaining it away, or hiding what it plainly says.

The dilemma assumes that Islam teaches only the first, and teaches it of the whole Bible. Most of the early scholars taught the second, and the rest held a mixture.

## What the commentators said {#commentators}

**Al-Ṭabarī** (d. 923), the first great commentator, explains Q 2:75 as an oral distortion: a deliberate bending of what God had said, with no tampering with the written text. On Q 5:48 he describes the Qur'an as *muʾtaman*, the trusted custodian over the books before it: whatever in them agrees with the Qur'an is true, and whatever disagrees with it is false.[1]

**Al-Qurṭubī** (d. 1273), commenting on the same verse, records three views: substitution in recitation; misinterpretation of clear passages, especially those describing the Prophet and the penalty for adultery; and rewriting of the text. He inclines to the first two.[2]

**Al-Māturīdī** (d. 944), on Q 4:46, takes "they shift words from their places" to mean attaching false interpretations to texts that still exist.[3]

**Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī** (d. 1210) gives perhaps the strongest classical argument against wholesale textual corruption. The Torah, he observes, was spread across East and West and recited by people of every kind. A text transmitted that widely, by so many people who could not all have colluded, could not be rewritten without the rewriting being known. The principle he applies, *tawātur*, mass transmission, is the same principle Muslims use to show the Qur'an's own preservation.[4]

These are not marginal writers. They are among the most read commentators in Islamic history, and none of them holds the position the dilemma assigns to "Islam".

## What modern scholars found {#modern}

Academic research on the question has reached the same conclusion from outside the tradition.

**Camilla Adang** surveyed nine major Muslim authors on the Hebrew Bible, from Ibn Rabbān al-Ṭabarī (d. c. 855) to Ibn Ḥazm (d. 1064). She found that most held a moderate view: the sense of the biblical text had been changed, while the text itself remained largely intact.[5]

**Thomas Michel**, surveying Muslim writing against Christianity more broadly, traced a standard line through ʿAlī al-Ṭabarī, the Zaydī theologian al-Qāsim ibn Ibrāhīm, al-Bāqillānī, al-Ghazālī and al-Rāzī. On that line, the Bible was treated as basically sound in its text and misread by Jews and Christians.[6]

**Gabriel Said Reynolds**, in the standard academic article on the subject, argues from the root *ḥ-r-f* ("to turn") and the word *mawāḍiʿ* ("places") that Q 4:46 is best translated "they shift words out of their contexts". On his reading, the Qur'an accuses its opponents of reading scripture out of context and of treating human words as revelation, and gives no clear basis for a charge of textual alteration.[7]

Ibn Ḥazm is the exception that shows the rule. He argued at length that the Torah and the Gospels had been textually altered, and he is the scholar most often named as the source of that view. But he was one voice, and the surveys show he was not the majority.

## Text or meaning? A refinement {#refinement}

The older picture, of an "early" charge of distorted meaning that "later" hardened into a charge of distorted text, has been refined. **Ryan Schaffner**, in a doctoral study of Muslim and Christian debate literature of the eighth and ninth centuries, examined al-Qāsim ibn Ibrāhīm, Ibn al-Layth, ʿAlī al-Ṭabarī, al-Jāḥiẓ and Ibn Qutaybah, alongside the Christian replies of Theodore Abū Qurrah, the patriarch Timothy I, ʿAmmār al-Baṣrī and Abraham of Tiberias.[8]

His finding is that the early Muslim writers used both charges together. They treated the Qur'an as the criterion. Biblical material that agreed with it was accepted and quoted; material that contradicted it was rejected as corrupted.

This does not weaken the Muslim case. It describes it. The classical method was neither "the Bible is sound" nor "the Bible is forged", but a third position: the Bible holds revelation and accretion together, and the Qur'an is the standard that tells them apart. That is exactly what Q 5:48 means when it calls the Qur'an *muhaymin*, guardian and arbiter over the earlier books. The dilemma offers two options. The tradition always worked with a third.

## The debate continued {#continued}

The question did not close in the Middle Ages, and later scholars ranged across the same positions.

- In the early eighteenth century the Naqshbandī scholar **Derviş Ali** wrote that he had once argued for textual falsification, had changed his mind, and now held that Jews and Christians had falsified the meaning, not the text.
- **Shāh Walī Allāh** of Delhi (d. 1762) rejected textual *taḥrīf* outright, while holding that Jews and Christians had misinterpreted revelation.
- **Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhāb** never applied *taḥrīf* to the Bible's text, and the editors of his work infer that he held the error to be one of interpretation.[9]

Jacques Waardenburg's survey of Muslim views of other religions records the full range: some held the text falsified, some that lines were omitted, and some that the text was reliable but misread.[10] The tradition was never one thing, which is the point. A dilemma that assumes it was is arguing with an invention.

## An older argument than it looks {#older}

The dilemma is presented as a new discovery. It is not. A letter of 1732 by the Christian writer **Isṭifān Ward**, known through a summary, argued that Q 3:84, 5:46 and 2:87 prove the authenticity of the Torah and Gospel, and that the Prophet would have exposed any falsification.[11] That is the dilemma's first half, nearly three centuries before its modern presentation.

Nor is the charge of scriptural falsification a Muslim invention. Christian writers had made it against Jewish interpreters for centuries before Islam, and the Qur'an's language takes up and redirects that older argument, as Nicolai Sinai has shown.[12] A Christian apologist who calls the very idea of *taḥrīf* absurd is rejecting a category his own tradition used first.

## "But that makes the Qur'an circular" {#circular}

A common follow-up objection is that the classical method is circular. If Muslims accept whatever in the Bible agrees with the Qur'an and reject whatever disagrees, then the Qur'an is the only real authority and the Bible is decoration.

The objection describes every later revelation's relation to an earlier one, including Christianity's own. Christians read the Hebrew scriptures through Christ. They set aside the food laws, treat the Levitical priesthood as fulfilled, regard the Temple sacrifices as replaced by the cross, and, in most churches, have moved the Sabbath. A later revelation decides which parts of the earlier one remain in force. If that is circular for Muslims, it is circular for Christians. Either both traditions may read earlier scripture through the later, or neither may. The dilemma permits it in Christian hands and forbids it in Muslim hands, and gives no reason for the difference.

The double standard has a history. In the second century the pagan philosopher **Celsus** asked Christians whether God had "condemned his own laws and changed his mind" when He sent Jesus to teach against Moses. His question survives in Origen's reply, *Against Celsus*.[13] The emperor **Julian** pressed the same argument two centuries later. Christian apologists answered it by developing precisely the method they now deny to Muslims: the later revelation judges what in the earlier one still binds.

## A Muslim scholar who quoted the Bible {#al-biqai}

One last case shows how far the tradition was from the dilemma's caricature. **Ibrāhīm ibn ʿUmar al-Biqāʿī** (d. 1480), a Mamluk-era commentator, jurist and hadith scholar, quoted the Torah and the Gospels so freely in his commentary on the Qur'an that a rival, al-Sakhāwī, accused him of "glorifying the Bible and debasing the Qur'an".[14]

Al-Biqāʿī replied that using the earlier scriptures was an old and respected Islamic practice. The Qur'an itself, the Prophet, the Companions and the leading scholars of every generation had referred to them. A Muslim scholar in 1480 was citing the Bible directly and defending the practice as orthodox, which is not what anyone would expect if the dilemma's picture of Islam were true.

## Why this answers the dilemma {#answer}

The dilemma has two branches: either the Bible is reliable and Islam is refuted, or the Bible is corrupted and the Qur'an affirmed a corrupted book. Both branches assume that Muslims must take the Bible whole, as wholly reliable or wholly corrupt.

The classical tradition never accepted that choice. Its majority view located the corruption mainly in meaning. Its working method read the Bible through the Qur'an and kept what agreed with it. Its most careful theologian argued that wholesale rewriting was impossible. And the Qur'an itself names the method, calling itself the *muhaymin* over what came before. The dilemma refutes a position Islam has not held.

!callout **In short.** The Qur'an uses *yuḥarrifūna* four times, always of certain Jews, never of Christians. Most classical scholars located *taḥrīf* in meaning, read the Bible through the Qur'an as criterion, and quoted what agreed with it. The dilemma's "wholly reliable or wholly corrupt" is a choice the tradition never accepted.
