title: Paul and the message of Jesus
excerpt: Paul never followed Jesus in his lifetime, says his gospel came by private revelation, clashed with Peter and James, and taught what James contradicts. The evidence comes from the New Testament itself.
order: 10

!lead Much of what Christians believe about salvation comes not from the Gospels but from the letters of Paul: that Jesus' death on the cross pays for the sins of the world, that the Law of Moses no longer binds believers, and that a person is justified by faith apart from works. Muslims have long argued that this teaching departs from the message of Jesus. This page sets out why, using only the New Testament, so that readers can check every step.

## Who Paul was {#who}

Paul, also called Saul, was a Jewish teacher from Tarsus who, by his own account, "persecuted the church of God violently and tried to destroy it" (Galatians 1:13). According to the Acts of the Apostles, he was travelling to Damascus to arrest Jesus' followers when he saw a light and heard a voice he identified as Jesus (Acts 9:1–9). He became a missionary, founded churches across the eastern Mediterranean, and wrote letters to them.

Those letters are the earliest Christian writings that survive. Most scholars date them to the 50s, a decade or more before Mark, the earliest Gospel. That matters. Paul's theology was in circulation before the Gospels were written, and it shaped how the Gospels were read. When Christians read the Gospels today, they usually read them through Paul.

## He never followed Jesus in his lifetime {#never-met}

Paul did not walk with Jesus during his ministry. He did not hear the Sermon on the Mount, see the healings or sit at the Last Supper. Everything he claims to know from Jesus directly, he claims through visions after Jesus was gone. He says so plainly:

> "For I would have you know, brothers, that the gospel that was preached by me is not man's gospel. For I did not receive it from any man, nor was I taught it, but I received it through a revelation of Jesus Christ." -- Galatians 1:11–12 · ESV

He adds that after this revelation, "I did not immediately consult with anyone; nor did I go up to Jerusalem to those who were apostles before me" (Galatians 1:16–17). He went to Arabia, then back to Damascus, and only three years later spent fifteen days with Peter.

Paul presents this as a strength: his gospel did not depend on the disciples. But from any other point of view it is a weakness. The disciples heard Jesus teach for years. Paul heard a voice on the road and taught for decades without their instruction. When Paul calls his message "my gospel" (Romans 2:16), the possessive is accurate.

## He clashed with the disciples {#clashed}

Paul's own letter records a public confrontation with Peter, whom the Gospels present as the leading disciple:

> "But when Cephas came to Antioch, I opposed him to his face, because he stood condemned." -- Galatians 2:11 · ESV

The dispute was about whether Gentile believers had to keep the Law, and Paul says Peter changed his behaviour "before certain men came from James" (Galatians 2:12). James, the brother of Jesus, led the community in Jerusalem. The followers of Jesus who knew him best were on the other side of the question from Paul.

The Acts of the Apostles, written by Paul's admirer, records the same tension years later. When Paul arrives in Jerusalem, James and the elders tell him:

> "You see, brother, how many thousands there are among the Jews of those who have believed. They are all zealous for the law, and they have been told about you that you teach all the Jews who are among the Gentiles to forsake Moses." -- Acts 21:20–21 · ESV

Thousands of believers in Jesus, led by his brother, were "zealous for the law". The report circulating about Paul was that he taught people to abandon it.

## Faith alone, or works? {#faith-works}

Paul and James answer the same question in opposite words:

> "For we hold that one is justified by faith apart from works of the law." -- Romans 3:28 · ESV

> "You see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone." -- James 2:24 · ESV

James even uses the same example Paul uses, Abraham, and draws the opposite conclusion (James 2:21–23; Romans 4:1–5). Christian theologians have offered many reconciliations of the two, and readers can weigh them. But on the face of the texts, the leader of the Jerusalem community and the apostle to the Gentiles disagreed on how a person is made right with God.

## The Law: fulfilled or ended? {#law}

Jesus, in the Gospel of Matthew, is emphatic about the Law of Moses:

> "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them. For truly, I say to you, until heaven and earth pass away, not an iota, not a dot, will pass from the Law until all is accomplished." -- Matthew 5:17–18 · ESV

He adds that whoever relaxes the least of the commandments and teaches others to do so will be called least in the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 5:19). When a man asks him what he must do to have eternal life, Jesus answers: "If you would enter life, keep the commandments" (Matthew 19:17), and lists them.

Paul writes that "Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes" {Rom 10:4}, and the letter to the Ephesians speaks of Christ "abolishing the law of commandments expressed in ordinances" (Ephesians 2:15). Christians explain these differently, but the plain contrast is hard to miss: Jesus says not a dot will pass from the Law and tells an enquirer to keep the commandments; Paul says Christ is the end of the Law.

## What Jesus taught about salvation {#jesus-salvation}

In the Gospels, Jesus' teaching on how to be saved is strikingly close to the Qur'an's. When asked which commandment is greatest, he answers with the declaration of God's oneness and the command to love God with all one's heart, and to love one's neighbour (Mark 12:28–31). The scribe who asked agrees with him, and Jesus tells him: "You are not far from the kingdom of God" (Mark 12:34). He teaches his followers to pray to God directly, to ask forgiveness, and to forgive others (Matthew 6:9–15). His parable of the returning son describes a father who forgives because his son came home (Luke 15:11–32).

None of these passages says that forgiveness depends on believing in a sacrifice. They describe God forgiving those who turn to Him, love Him and obey Him. That is the message the Qur'an attributes to Jesus, and to every prophet.

## "All things to all people" {#all-things}

Paul describes his own method in a way that has troubled readers for centuries:

> "To the Jews I became as a Jew, in order to win Jews. To those under the law I became as one under the law (though not being myself under the law) that I might win those under the law. ... I have become all things to all people, that by all means I might save some." -- 1 Corinthians 9:20–22 · ESV

Christians read this as missionary flexibility. Muslims read it as a man adapting his message to his audience, which is exactly what the disciples in Jerusalem seem to have feared. Either way, it is not the description of someone handing on unchanged what he had received from Jesus.

## No prophet between Jesus and Muhammad {#no-prophet}

The Muslim objection to Paul goes deeper than the scholarly debate about which letters he wrote. The prior question is whether he had the standing to bring revelation at all. The Prophet answered it:

::hadith أَنَا أَوْلَى النَّاسِ بِابْنِ مَرْيَمَ، وَالأَنْبِيَاءُ أَوْلاَدُ عَلاَّتٍ، لَيْسَ بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَهُ نَبِيٌّ

"I am the closest of people to the son of Mary. The prophets are brothers of one father, and there is no prophet between me and him" (*Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* 3442; *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* 2365). In another wording: "their mothers are different, and their religion is one" (*Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* 3443).

Paul claimed exactly the kind of standing this rules out. He called himself an apostle sent "through Jesus Christ and God the Father" {Gal 1:1}, "not in the least inferior to these super-apostles" {2 Cor 11:5}, whose gospel came not from any man: "I received it through a revelation of Jesus Christ" {Gal 1:11–12}. If Jesus is God, a revelation from him after his death is a revelation from God. If Jesus is God's human messenger, as the Qur'an says, and no prophet came between him and Muhammad, the claim has no place.

Christian apologists sometimes answer that Paul belongs among the *ḥawāriyyūn*, the disciples of Jesus whom the Qur'an praises {Q 3:52 · 5:111 · 61:14}. But the Qur'an's *ḥawāriyyūn* are the companions who declared their faith to Jesus in person, during his ministry. Paul, by his own account, never met him. What Paul claimed was not discipleship but revelation, and that is the office the hadith closes. The case is set out at length in Mohd Elfie Nieshaem Juferi, *Paulus: Perosak Risalah al-Masīḥ* (Seri Kembangan: Langgam Fikir, 2025), 134–159, and summarised in [*Reconsidering the "Islamic Dilemma"*](https://www.academia.edu/166160379/Reconsidering_The_Islamic_Dilemma), section VIII.

## What Muslims say, and do not say {#muslim-view}

The Qur'an does not name Paul. Muslims do not need to settle every question about him to make the point that matters: the question "what did Jesus teach?" cannot be answered from Paul's letters, because Paul did not learn from Jesus during his ministry and disagreed with those who did. Medieval Muslim scholars made this argument long before modern critics of Paul. Some, such as the theologian ʿAbd al-Jabbār, argued that the Christianity of their day owed more to Paul and the later councils than to Jesus.[1]

Muslims also do not claim that Paul was insincere, or that Christians who follow his letters are acting in bad faith. Most Christians have never been told that the New Testament records these disputes. We set them out so that readers can look at the texts and draw their own conclusions.

## A Christian objection, and a reply {#objection}

Christians reply that Paul's authority was recognised by the other apostles. In Galatians, Paul says that James, Peter and John "gave the right hand of fellowship" to him and Barnabas (Galatians 2:9), and the second letter of Peter refers to Paul's letters alongside "the other Scriptures" (2 Peter 3:16).

Both points deserve an answer. The handshake in Galatians 2:9 is reported by Paul himself, in the same chapter in which he describes opposing Peter to his face; it is evidence of an agreement about where each would preach, not of agreement on every question. And 2 Peter was itself one of the books whose place in the New Testament was disputed in the early church, and many scholars, including many Christian scholars, regard it as written after Peter's death. Neither passage removes the disagreements Paul's own letters record.

## An invitation {#invitation}

The Qur'an's answer to the question "what did Jesus teach?" is that he taught what every prophet taught: worship the One God, obey Him, and turn to Him for forgiveness. "The Messiah said: Children of Israel, worship God, my Lord and your Lord" {Q 5:72}. The Gospels' own record of Jesus' words, read without Paul's letters as a lens, says much the same.

If you are a Christian and these passages trouble you, we invite you to read the Gospels again, slowly, asking only what Jesus himself said. Then read what the Qur'an says about him.
