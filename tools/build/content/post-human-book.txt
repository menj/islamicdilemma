!lead A frequently asked question on one Christian apologetics site asks whether Christians believe the Qur'an is from God. Its answer is short: "We see the Quran as a human book that contains many serious errors about God, Christ, and the gospel—even while it says some true things about earlier revelation." No error is named, and none is shown.

The statement is offered as a conclusion. This article asks what it would take to establish it, and whether the examples usually offered meet that standard.

## A disagreement is not an error {#disagreement}

Look at the three subjects of the claimed errors: God, Christ and the gospel. On each, the Qur'an says something different from Christian doctrine.

It says God is one and has no son {Q 112:1–4}, that Jesus was a servant and messenger of God {Q 19:30}, and that he was not crucified {Q 4:157}. Christians believe otherwise.

But to call these *errors* is to assume the point in dispute. It measures the Qur'an by Christian doctrine and declares it wrong wherever it differs.

A Muslim could do the same in reverse: measure the New Testament by the Qur'an and call every difference an error. Neither proves anything. The question between the two books is which of them is the true standard, and that question cannot be settled by assuming the answer.

The Qur'an is explicit that it differs from Christian doctrine, and says why. It calls itself *muhaymin*, guardian over the earlier scripture {Q 5:48}, confirming what is true in it and correcting what is not.

A book that announces it will correct earlier teaching is not making an error when it does so. It is doing what it says it will do. The argument about which correction is right belongs to the evidence on each subject, which this site sets out: on [the oneness of God and the Son](/son-of-god-and-quran-5-116/), on [Jesus' own words](/did-jesus-claim-to-be-god/), and on [the crucifixion](/quran-4-157-and-history-of-crucifixion/).

## What an error would be {#what-error}

An error, in the sense that would show a book is merely human, is a statement that contradicts something established apart from the dispute. That means one of two things:

- An internal contradiction: the book says one thing in one place and its opposite in another, on the same subject and in the same sense.
- A factual mistake: the book says something about history, language or the world that outside evidence shows to be false.

The Qur'an invites exactly this test:

::ayah Q 4:82 | أَفَلَا يَتَدَبَّرُونَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ ۚ وَلَوْ كَانَ مِنْ عِندِ غَيْرِ ٱللَّهِ لَوَجَدُوا۟ فِيهِ ٱخْتِلَـٰفًۭا كَثِيرًۭا | Do they not consider the Qur'an (with care)? Had it been from other Than Allah, they would surely have found therein Much discrepancy.

A book that challenges its readers to find contradictions in it is not hiding from scrutiny. Muslims have taken the challenge seriously for centuries.

In the ninth century Ibn Qutaybah wrote a whole book on passages that readers found difficult, *Taʾwīl Mushkil al-Qurʾān*, and later scholars such as al-Zarkashī devoted chapters to verses that appear, at first reading, to conflict.[1] The tradition did not suppress the questions. It answered them in writing.

## A worked example: "sister of Aaron" {#sister-of-aaron}

The most famous alleged factual error in the Qur'an is in Sūrat Maryam. When Mary returns to her people with the infant Jesus, they say:

::ayah Q 19:28 | يَـٰٓأُخْتَ هَـٰرُونَ مَا كَانَ أَبُوكِ ٱمْرَأَ سَوْءٍۢ وَمَا كَانَتْ أُمُّكِ بَغِيًّۭا | "O sister of Aaron! Thy father was not a man of evil, nor thy mother a woman unchaste!"

The objection is that the Qur'an has confused Mary, the mother of Jesus, with Miriam, the sister of Moses and Aaron, who lived more than a thousand years earlier.

The objection is not new. It was put to a companion of the Prophet by Christians in his own lifetime.

*Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* records that al-Mughīrah ibn Shuʿbah was sent to Najrān, where Christians asked him: "You recite 'sister of Aaron', yet Moses lived such and such a time before Jesus." He brought the question to the Prophet, who answered:

::hadith إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا يُسَمُّونَ بِأَنْبِيَائِهِمْ وَالصَّالِحِينَ قَبْلَهُمْ

"They used to name themselves after their prophets and the righteous people who came before them."[2]

The answer is a point about language that the New Testament itself illustrates. Luke introduces the mother of John the Baptist in exactly the same way:

> "And he had a wife from the daughters of Aaron, and her name was Elizabeth." -- Luke 1:5 · ESV

Elizabeth was not literally Aaron's daughter. She was of the priestly line descended from him.

"Daughter of Aaron" in Luke and "sister of Aaron" in the Qur'an use the same idiom: belonging to the line or the household of Aaron. Mary's family, the Qur'an says elsewhere, was "the family of 'Imran" {Q 3:33–35}, the priestly house, and Luke calls Elizabeth Mary's relative {Luke 1:36}. The phrase places Mary in a priestly family known for its righteousness, which is exactly the point her people are making: how could a woman of such a family come home with a child?

So the most famous "error" was raised by Christians fourteen centuries ago, answered by the Prophet himself, and is answered again by the Gospel of Luke. That does not prove the Qur'an is from God.

It shows that the claim of "many serious errors" cannot be made by assertion. Each alleged error has to be examined, and when the best-known ones are examined, they turn out to be misreadings.

## "Borrowed from other books" is not an error either {#borrowed}

A second kind of objection says that some Qur'anic stories also appear in other writings, and concludes that the Prophet copied them. The Qur'an says Jesus made birds of clay that flew by God's permission {Q 5:110}; a similar story appears in a Christian infancy gospel outside the New Testament.

But a story's appearing in another book does not make it false. The New Testament itself quotes the Book of Enoch {Jude 14–15} and names Pharaoh's magicians, Jannes and Jambres, who appear nowhere in the Old Testament {2 Tim 3:8}.

Christians do not conclude that Jude or Paul made an error. The Qur'an's position is that God revealed to the Prophet what happened, including events remembered, in part, in other traditions {Q 3:44}.

Whether one accepts that is a question about revelation. It is not evidence of a mistake.

## The argument the dilemma itself makes {#dilemma}

There is something odd about calling the Qur'an "a human book that contains many serious errors" on a site devoted to the Islamic Dilemma. The dilemma depends on reading the Qur'an with great care.

It quotes the Qur'an's exact words about the Torah and the Gospel, builds a syllogism on them, and treats their consistency as the question. An argument that takes the Qur'an's wording that seriously cannot also wave it away as careless.

And when the dilemma's own verses are read in full, they turn out to be consistent. The Qur'an affirms the revelation given to Moses and Jesus, says parts of what their communities held were altered or forgotten, and places itself as guardian over the rest.

That is one position, stated across many verses, without contradiction. The supposed inconsistency appears only when half of each passage is quoted.

## What a merely human author would have to explain {#human-author}

A reader who concludes that the Qur'an is a human book still has to account for what that book is. Muslims point to several features, and readers can weigh them.

- Its author. The Qur'an describes the Prophet as *ummī*, unlettered {Q 7:157}, a man with no reputation as a poet or scholar before the age of forty, who then recited a text the Arabs of his time, masters of their language, could not match.
- Its challenge. It asks anyone in doubt to "produce a Sura like thereunto" {Q 2:23}, a challenge made publicly to the people best placed to meet it, which Muslims hold was never met.
- Its preservation. It was memorised and written in the Prophet's lifetime, gathered within two years of his death, and standardised within a generation, as [the history of its text](/qiraat-and-preservation-of-quran/) shows.
- Its coherence. Revealed piece by piece over twenty-three years, in response to events, it presents one consistent account of God, the prophets and the Last Day.

None of these is a proof that compels assent. Faith in Islam is reached by evidence weighed freely. But they are the facts a "human book" explanation has to explain, and simply calling the book human does not explain them.

## How to answer the claim when you meet it {#answer}

When someone says the Qur'an contains "many serious errors", the right response is a single question: which one? Ask for a specific verse and a specific fact, and examine them together. In most conversations the claimed errors turn out to be of three kinds:

1. Disagreements with Christian doctrine, presented as errors. These are real disagreements and deserve discussion, but they are not mistakes.
2. Misreadings, such as "sister of Aaron", or the claim that Q 5:116 puts Mary in the Trinity. It does not mention the Trinity at all; see ["He does not beget"](/son-of-god-and-quran-5-116/).
3. Verses quoted in part. The [errors in the case for the dilemma](/errors-in-case-for-dilemma/) are mostly of this kind.

The Qur'an does not ask to be believed without examination. It asks to be examined. "Do they not reflect on the Qur'an?" is a question addressed to every reader, including the one who has already decided the answer.
