title: Beyond the Gospels: do the letters call Jesus God?

!lead Christian apologists who concede that Jesus never says "I am God, worship me" in the Gospels often reply that the claim does not wait for the Gospels. The letters, they say, state it directly and early: Jesus "in the form of God" {Phil 2:6}, the "fullness of deity" dwelling in him {Col 2:9}, "our great God and Savior" {Titus 2:13}, "your throne, O God" {Heb 1:8}, and "the first and the last" {Rev 1:17}. This article reads each passage with the verses around it, in the ESV the apologists themselves use, and shows what they leave out.

## The method {#method}

The Gospel passages are examined in [Did Jesus claim to be God?](/did-jesus-claim-to-be-god/). The same method applies here. For each passage, we read the sentence before and after, and ask whether the same author says anything elsewhere that settles how he meant it. A writer who believed Jesus was God in the full sense would not be expected to say, in the same breath, that Jesus has a God, that God gave him his honour, or that he will be subject to God at the end.

## Philippians 2:6–11: exalted by God {#philippians}

> "who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men." -- Philippians 2:6–7 · ESV

This hymn is the passage most often quoted. Christian scholars themselves debate what "in the form of God" means, and whether "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped" refers to equality he had and gave up, or equality he never reached for. The sentence can be read either way.

What is not debatable is how the passage ends:

> "Therefore God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name, so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father." -- Philippians 2:9–11 · ESV

The subject of the exaltation is God. God exalted him, "therefore", because of his obedience. God *bestowed* the name on him. And the confession that follows is "to the glory of God the Father". A being who is God does not receive exaltation from God as a reward for obedience, and the worship the passage describes is directed, in the end, to the Father. Apologists point out that "every knee should bow" echoes Isaiah 45:23, where every knee bows to the LORD. Paul's own ending answers that: the bowing ends in the glory of God the Father.

## Colossians 1 and 2: "the fullness of God was pleased to dwell" {#colossians}

> "He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation." -- Colossians 1:15 · ESV

The passage goes on to say that all things were created "by him" and "through him". But it begins by calling him the image of the invisible God, a likeness, not the original, and "the firstborn of all creation", a phrase that places him within creation, first in rank.

Then it says why he has the place he has:

> "For in him all the fullness of God was pleased to dwell" -- Colossians 1:19 · ESV

The fullness of God *was pleased* to dwell in him. It is God's choice to dwell in him, not his own nature. The same idea returns in the next chapter, "in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily" {Col 2:9}, and the very next verse applies the same language to the believers: "and you have been filled in him" {Col 2:10}. The related letter to the Ephesians prays that its readers will be "filled with all the fullness of God" {Eph 3:19}. If being filled with God's fullness made someone God, it would make the readers of these letters God too. The language describes God's presence and favour, not identity with Him.

## Titus 2:13 and 2 Peter 1:1: one person or two? {#titus}

> "waiting for our blessed hope, the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" -- Titus 2:13 · ESV

The ESV reads "our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" as one person. The Greek can also be read as two, and for centuries the most widely used English Bible did exactly that:

> "Looking for that blessed hope, and the glorious appearing of the great God and our Saviour Jesus Christ" -- Titus 2:13 · KJV

The difference rests on a disputed point of Greek grammar, and the King James translators, who believed in the Trinity, still rendered it as two persons. The same is true of 2 Peter 1:1, which the KJV renders "the righteousness of God and our Saviour Jesus Christ". In 2 Peter the next verse settles the question by distinguishing the two plainly:

> "May grace and peace be multiplied to you in the knowledge of God and of Jesus our Lord." -- 2 Peter 1:2 · ESV

A verse whose meaning depends on a contested grammatical rule, and which the most famous Trinitarian translation renders against the apologists' reading, cannot carry the weight of a doctrine. Where the same letter speaks unambiguously, it speaks of "God and Jesus our Lord".

## Hebrews 1:8: "Your throne, O God" {#hebrews}

> "But of the Son he says, 'Your throne, O God, is forever and ever, the scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom.'" -- Hebrews 1:8 · ESV

Hebrews is quoting Psalm 45. That psalm is a royal wedding song, addressed to a king of Israel: "I address my verses to the king" {Ps 45:1}. In its original setting, "Your throne, O God" is said to a human king, in the court language of the ancient Near East, where kings were addressed with divine honour. Nobody reads the psalm as saying that the king of Israel was the God of Israel.

And Hebrews does not stop at verse 8. It quotes the next line of the psalm too:

> "You have loved righteousness and hated wickedness; therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions." -- Hebrews 1:9 · ESV

The one addressed as "O God" has a God, "God, your God", who anoints him and sets him above his companions. Hebrews itself describes Jesus, "in the days of his flesh", offering "prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death" {Heb 5:7}. The author who quotes "O God" is the same author who shows Jesus praying to God to save him.

## Revelation 1:17: "the first and the last" {#revelation}

> "Fear not, I am the first and the last, and the living one. I died, and behold I am alive forevermore" -- Revelation 1:17–18 · ESV

In Isaiah, the LORD says "I am the first and I am the last" {Isa 44:6}. Apologists argue that Revelation gives Jesus the divine title. But Revelation also explains its own terms. Its first verse says where its message comes from:

> "The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show to his servants the things that must soon take place." -- Revelation 1:1 · ESV

God *gave* it to him. And the risen Christ of Revelation speaks of "my God" four times in a single verse:

> "The one who conquers, I will make him a pillar in the temple of my God. Never shall he go out of it, and I will write on him the name of my God, and the name of the city of my God, the new Jerusalem, which comes down from my God out of heaven, and my own new name." -- Revelation 3:12 · ESV

Two verses later he is called "the beginning of God's creation" {Rev 3:14}. Whatever "the first and the last" means in Revelation 1, it is said by one who has a God, who receives his revelation from God, and who is described as the beginning of God's creation. The passage itself also marks the difference: "I died". The God of Isaiah does not die.

## What the same letters say plainly {#plainly}

Against these contested passages stand statements in the same letters that are not contested at all.

> "For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" -- 1 Timothy 2:5 · ESV

> "When all things are subjected to him, then the Son himself will also be subjected to him who put all things in subjection under him, that God may be all in all." -- 1 Corinthians 15:28 · ESV

And Paul's own confession of faith, often quoted for the Trinity, says: "for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things" {1 Cor 8:6}. One God, the Father; one mediator, the man Christ Jesus; the Son subjected to God at the end, "that God may be all in all". These are the plainest statements in the letters about how God and Jesus are related. The doctrine of the Trinity was formulated in the fourth century in part to reconcile them with the passages examined above. The letters themselves never state it.

## How the Qur'an describes the same person {#quran}

The Qur'an's description of Jesus matches the letters' plain statements and the Gospels' record of his own words.

::ayah Q 5:75 | مَّا ٱلْمَسِيحُ ٱبْنُ مَرْيَمَ إِلَّا رَسُولٌۭ قَدْ خَلَتْ مِن قَبْلِهِ ٱلرُّسُلُ | The Messiah, son of Mary, was only a messenger; messengers passed away before him.

It calls him "a word from God" and "a spirit from Him" {Q 4:171}, honours that the letters' high language echoes. But it states that he was God's servant and messenger, that God is one, and that "it is not fitting for the Most Merciful to take a son" {Q 19:92}. The letters' exalted titles, read with their own qualifications, describe a figure honoured *by* God. The Qur'an says the same, and says where the honour ends.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

The letters do use exalted language about Jesus. But every passage cited for his deity carries its own limit in the same text:

- God exalted him and gave him the name, to the glory of the Father {Phil 2:9–11}.
- The fullness of God was pleased to dwell in him, as it fills the believers {Col 1:19 · 2:10}.
- The grammar of "God and Savior" is disputed, and the next verse distinguishes God from Jesus {2 Pet 1:2}.
- "O God" is a royal psalm, followed by "God, your God, has anointed you" {Heb 1:9}.
- The one who is "the first and the last" speaks of "my God" four times {Rev 3:12}.

The letters do not close the gap the Gospels leave open. They widen it.

!callout **In short.** Each passage cited from the letters comes with its own qualification: God exalted him, God's fullness chose to dwell in him, "God, your God, has anointed you", "my God" four times. The plainest lines in the same letters say "one God, the Father" and "the man Christ Jesus".
