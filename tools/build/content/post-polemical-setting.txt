!lead The Islamic Dilemma reads the Qur'an's verses about the Torah and the Gospel as if they were articles of a creed: formal declarations that the Bible, in its present form, is vouched for by God. That is not how the verses work. They are spoken into an argument, with particular Jewish and Christian communities in seventh-century Arabia, and they do what arguments do: they hold the other side to its own professed standards. This article reads them in that setting, following section X of [*Reconsidering the "Islamic Dilemma"*](https://www.academia.edu/166160379/Reconsidering_The_Islamic_Dilemma).

## An argument, not a certificate {#argument}

The pattern is consistent across the Medinan chapters. The People of the Book are addressed as people who already have genuine revelation. They are then charged with neglecting it, with preferring other standards to it, and with using their attachment to it as a reason to reject the new revelation.

- Of the Christians, the Qur'an says they "forgot a portion of what they were reminded of" {Q 5:14}.
- Of a party of the Jews, it says they heard the word of God and "distorted it after they had understood it" {Q 2:75}.
- It asks whether they want "the judgment of the time of ignorance" instead of God's {Q 5:50}.
- It describes some who came to the Prophet for judgment only to avoid the ruling in their own book {Q 5:41–43}.

In this setting, the commands to "uphold the Torah and the Gospel" or to "judge by what God revealed therein" are challenges to consistency. They say: if you truly honoured what was sent to you, you would recognise what is now being sent. A sincere follower of the earlier revelation, looking honestly at what remained of it, would accept the new one. That those addressed did not is, in the Qur'an's argument, a charge against them, not against the revelation they claimed to follow. The passage is read in detail in [Judge by it, uphold it](/judge-by-it-uphold-it/).

## Who was listening {#audience}

The setting has a second side. Most of the Prophet's first hearers were not Jews or Christians but pagan Arabs, for whom the stories of Adam, Moses and Aaron, the Exodus, and Jesus and his disciples were unfamiliar. When the Qur'an said it confirmed what had been given to the Jews and Christians, it told those hearers something they needed to know: that the stories now being recited stood in continuity with revelations their monotheist neighbours already had.

The language of confirmation authenticated the new revelation by pointing to the old. Reading the same words as a guarantee of the text of a modern critical edition of the Bible is, in the strict sense, an anachronism.

## The Torah of Medina {#medina-torah}

The hadith collections show this argument in practice. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim record that Jews of Medina brought a man and a woman accused of adultery to the Prophet, hoping for a lighter penalty than their own law required. When the Torah was read, the reader covered the verse of stoning with his hand, and ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām, a former rabbi, told him to lift it.[1] The incident, examined in [Judge by it, uphold it](/judge-by-it-uphold-it/), shows God's ruling still present in the Torah and some of its keepers concealing it.

A related narration in the *Sunan* of Abū Dāwūd adds a detail. Two Jewish scholars, asked what the Torah said, replied that if four witnesses testify that they saw the act itself, "like the kohl stick in the kohl pot", the two are stoned.[2]

::hadith نَجِدُ فِي التَّوْرَاةِ إِذَا شَهِدَ أَرْبَعَةٌ أَنَّهُمْ رَأَوْا ذَكَرَهُ فِي فَرْجِهَا مِثْلَ الْمِيلِ فِي الْمُكْحُلَةِ رُجِمَا

No such rule is in the Hebrew Bible held today. The Torah requires the death penalty for adultery {Lev 20:10 · Deut 22:22} and, in capital cases generally, two or three witnesses {Deut 17:6}, but it says nothing of four witnesses or of the standard of evidence those scholars quoted. If the narration is sound, the Torah they recited in seventh-century Medina contained material that the Masoretic text does not. The hadith scholars differ on the narration's chain: al-Albānī graded it sound, while Shuʿayb al-Arnaʾūṭ graded it weak. It should be held with that caution.

## The cushion {#cushion}

Christian apologists often quote another narration of the same event, also in Abū Dāwūd. In it, the Jews of Medina placed a cushion for the Prophet; he took it from beneath him, set the Torah on it and said: "I believe in you and in the One who revealed you."[3]

::hadith آمَنْتُ بِكِ وَبِمَنْ أَنْزَلَكِ

The apologist's conclusion is that the Prophet endorsed the Torah of his day, entire. Three points apply.

**First, the words are found in this one narration.** The accounts of the same incident in al-Bukhārī and Muslim do not contain them. Al-Albānī graded this narration *ḥasan*, acceptable, so a Muslim should not dismiss it, but its wording should not be stretched beyond what it says.

**Second, what it honours is the Torah as revelation.** "I believe in you and in the One who revealed you" is a statement of faith that the Torah was revealed by God, which every Muslim affirms. It says nothing about whether every word in that scroll was transmitted without loss.

**Third, the Torah honoured in that room was the Torah of the Jews of Medina at that moment.** On the apologist's own reading, that Torah is the one the Prophet affirmed, and the narrations of the same event show it containing material the Bible of today lacks. An argument that uses the cushion narration to prove the Prophet endorsed the modern Bible is using a scroll that, by the same evidence, was not the modern Bible.

## "The Torah and the Gospel are with the Jews and Christians" {#tirmidhi}

A report in al-Tirmidhī is also quoted as evidence that the Prophet recognised the Torah and Gospel of his day. Abū al-Dardāʾ relates that the Prophet looked up to the sky and said that a time was coming when knowledge would be snatched away from people. Ziyād ibn Labīd asked how that could be, when they recited the Qur'an and taught it to their wives and children. The Prophet answered:

::hadith هَذِهِ التَّوْرَاةُ وَالإِنْجِيلُ عِنْدَ الْيَهُودِ وَالنَّصَارَى فَمَاذَا تُغْنِي عَنْهُمْ

"Here are the Torah and the Gospel with the Jews and the Christians: what good do they do them?"[4]

The point of the saying is not the Bible's reliability. It is a warning to Muslims: possessing a book and reciting it is worth nothing without understanding and practice. The companion ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit, asked about it, said that the first knowledge to be lifted from people would be humility in prayer. The word *ʿinda*, "with", places the books in seventh-century hands; it does not certify their contents. And the sense matches the Qur'an's own image of those who were given the Torah and did not carry it {Q 62:5}. The report is neutral on *taḥrīf*, and its force runs against the idea that possessing a scripture guarantees anything.

## Neither believe nor disbelieve {#bukhari}

The Prophet's own instruction to his companions sets the posture for all such material. Abū Hurayrah relates that the People of the Book used to read the Torah in Hebrew and explain it in Arabic to the Muslims, and the Prophet said:

::hadith لاَ تُصَدِّقُوا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ وَلاَ تُكَذِّبُوهُمْ وَقُولُوا آمَنَّا بِاللَّهِ وَمَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْنَا

"Do not believe the People of the Book and do not disbelieve them, but say: We believe in God and in what was sent down to us."[5]

Ibn Ḥajar explains the reason: what they relate may carry genuine revelation alongside human additions, and without a criterion they cannot be told apart.[6] The criterion is the Qur'an. That is neither wholesale acceptance nor wholesale rejection, and it is not the binary the dilemma demands.

## Matthew 23: "do whatever they tell you" {#matthew-23}

The Gospels contain a close parallel to the Qur'an's way of speaking:

> "The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses' seat, so do and observe whatever they tell you, but not the works they do. For they preach, but do not practice." -- Matthew 23:2–3 · ESV

Taken at face value, "whatever they tell you" would commit Jesus to every doctrine the Pharisees taught, including the traditions he condemns at length in the rest of the same chapter. No Christian reads it that way. The usual reading is that Jesus affirms the Pharisees' authority to teach the Law of Moses without endorsing every position they held.

That is exactly the reading Muslims give the Qur'an's command to "uphold the Torah and the Gospel". If "whatever they tell you" can be read in context, so can "judge by what God revealed therein". To grant the first and refuse the second is a double standard, and the dilemma depends on it.

## One position, three parts {#synthesis}

Three claims make up the Muslim answer, and they fit together:

1. **The Qur'an's charge of *taḥrīf* is aimed chiefly at oral and interpretive distortion**, as the classical commentators on Q 2:75 and 4:46 and modern scholars such as Gabriel Said Reynolds explain. See [What classical Muslim scholars actually said about taḥrīf](/what-classical-muslim-scholars-said-about-tahrif/).
2. **As *muhaymin*, the Qur'an still corrects a false doctrine wherever it appears**, whether in oral teaching or in a written text, because judging a doctrine reaches it in every form.
3. **The manuscripts of both Testaments show the layering and change the Qur'an's account leads one to expect.** See [The Hebrew Bible before the Masoretic text](/hebrew-bible-before-masoretic-text/).

An apologist who sets one of these against another, for instance "you say the problem is interpretation, so you cannot object to the text", has misread the Muslim position as a set of fragments. It is one position.

## Q 50:38: "no weariness touched Us" {#weariness}

One pair of verses shows the *muhaymin* principle at work. The Qur'an says:

::ayah Q 50:38 | وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا فِى سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍۢ وَمَا مَسَّنَا مِن لُّغُوبٍۢ | We created the heavens and the earth and everything between them in six days, and no weariness touched Us.

The Torah says God "rested" on the seventh day {Gen 2:2}, and Exodus adds that "on the seventh day he rested and was refreshed" {Exod 31:17}. The Hebrew verb, *wayyinnāpash*, means to catch one's breath.

Apologists sometimes argue that because the Qur'an corrects the biblical text here, it must assume that text was fixed. The conclusion does not follow. The Qur'an corrects a teaching, that God tires, and the correction holds wherever the teaching is found: in the written text, in its interpretation, or in both. That is what it means to be *muhaymin*.

## Q 4:157: "their saying" {#4-157}

The same applies to the crucifixion:

::ayah Q 4:157 | وَقَوْلِهِمْ إِنَّا قَتَلْنَا ٱلْمَسِيحَ عِيسَى ٱبْنَ مَرْيَمَ رَسُولَ ٱللَّهِ وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ وَمَا صَلَبُوهُ وَلَـٰكِن شُبِّهَ لَهُمْ ۚ | And for their saying, "We killed the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, the messenger of God". They did not kill him and they did not crucify him; it was made to appear so to them.

Some apologists argue that because the verse rebukes "their saying", the boast of certain Jews, it corrects only that boast, and leaves the Gospels' account of the crucifixion untouched and even endorsed.

But what the verse denies is a fact: that Jesus was killed and crucified. That claim appears in the boast the verse quotes, and it appears in all four Gospels. A claim corrected in one place is corrected wherever it is made. The word "their saying" identifies whom the verse first answers; it does not limit what the verse denies. The history behind the verse is examined in [Q 4:157 and the history of the crucifixion](/quran-4-157-and-history-of-crucifixion/).

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

Read as detached statements, the Qur'an's verses about the Torah and the Gospel can be made to sound like an endorsement of the modern Bible. Read in their setting, they are what they appear to be: an argument with the People of the Book, holding them to their own scripture, while the Qur'an judges what in that scripture still stands. The hadith confirm this posture, the Gospels contain a parallel, and the Qur'an's corrections of specific teachings show it in operation.

!callout **In short.** The "confirmation" verses are moves in an argument with the Jews and Christians of Medina, holding them to their own standards. The cushion narration honours the Torah as revelation and, on the apologist's own reading, the Torah of Medina, not the modern Bible. "Do and observe whatever they tell you" {Matt 23:3} is read in context by Christians; the Qur'an deserves the same reading.
