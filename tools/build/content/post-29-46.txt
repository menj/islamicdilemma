!lead The Islamic Dilemma often opens with Q 29:46. Muslims, it says, are commanded to tell Christians "we believe in what was revealed to you, and our God and your God is one." Yet many Muslims say the opposite: "we don't believe in your book because it has been corrupted, and your God is a false God."

The argument presents this as a contradiction between the Qur'an and Muslim belief. It is worth reading the verse in full, and asking whose position is actually being contradicted.

::ayah Q 29:46 | ۞ وَلَا تُجَـٰدِلُوٓا۟ أَهْلَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ إِلَّا بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا۟ مِنْهُمْ ۖ وَقُولُوٓا۟ ءَامَنَّا بِٱلَّذِىٓ أُنزِلَ إِلَيْنَا وَأُنزِلَ إِلَيْكُمْ وَإِلَـٰهُنَا وَإِلَـٰهُكُمْ وَٰحِدٌۭ وَنَحْنُ لَهُۥ مُسْلِمُونَ | And dispute ye not with the People of the Book, except with means better (than mere disputation), unless it be with those of them who inflict wrong (and injury): but say, "We believe in the revelation which has come down to us and in that which came down to you; Our Allah and your Allah is one; and it is to Him we bow (in Islam)."

## The first half of the verse {#first-half}

The argument usually quotes the second half of the verse. The first half sets its terms: "Do not argue with the People of the Book except in the best way."

The verse is about how Muslims should conduct discussion with Jews and Christians: courteously, on common ground, beginning from what both sides affirm. It is not a doctrinal statement about the textual condition of the Bible. It is an instruction about conversation, and its second half supplies the words with which Muslims are to open it.

That context matters for two reasons. It tells Muslims how they should engage with arguments like the Islamic Dilemma: calmly, without insult, starting from shared belief.

And it tells us what the declaration that follows is for. It is a statement of common ground, not a concession that whatever a community holds is revelation.

## "What was sent down to you" {#sent-down}

The words Muslims are told to say are precise: *āmannā bi-lladhī unzila ilaynā wa-unzila ilaykum*, "we believe in what was sent down to us and what was sent down to you." The object of belief is what God *sent down*, the revelation itself.

It is not "the books you now hold", or "everything your scholars have written". It is the revelation God gave.

The Qur'an spells out what this means in a fuller version of the same declaration:

::ayah Q 2:136 | قُولُوٓا۟ ءَامَنَّا بِٱللَّهِ وَمَآ أُنزِلَ إِلَيْنَا وَمَآ أُنزِلَ إِلَىٰٓ إِبْرَٰهِـۧمَ وَإِسْمَـٰعِيلَ وَإِسْحَـٰقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ وَٱلْأَسْبَاطِ وَمَآ أُوتِىَ مُوسَىٰ وَعِيسَىٰ | Say ye: "We believe in Allah, and the revelation given to us, and to Abraham, Isma'il, Isaac, Jacob, and the Tribes, and that given to Moses and Jesus…

"What was given to Moses and Jesus." The belief is in what the prophets received.

Muslims affirm that fully. The dilemma reads "what was sent down to you" as "the Bible in your hands", but that is not what either verse says.

## How the Prophet taught Muslims to apply it {#hadith}

The Prophet explained how Muslims should treat what the People of the Book reported from their scripture. When Jews in Medina read the Torah in Hebrew and explained it in Arabic to the Muslims, he said:

!callout "Do not believe the People of the Book and do not disbelieve them, but say: We believe in God and what was revealed to us." [1]

This is the posture the verse describes. Muslims are not to accept every report from the earlier scriptures as true, because some of it may have been altered.

Nor are they to reject every report as false, because some of it may be genuine revelation. They are to affirm their belief in what God revealed, and suspend judgment on what cannot be verified against the Qur'an. That is exactly the *muhaymin* relationship {Q 5:48}: earlier scripture is neither wholly accepted nor wholly rejected; it is tested.

The argument presents Muslims with two choices, belief or disbelief in the Bible. The Prophet taught a third, and taught it in the context of this very verse.

## "Our God and your God is one" {#one-god}

The second claim attached to this verse is that Muslims contradict the Qur'an when they say the Christian God is false. Here the argument is right that some Muslims speak carelessly. But the Qur'an's position is clear, and it is not the one the argument attributes to Muslims.

The Qur'an says, "our God and your God is one." Muslims believe that Jews and Christians worship the God of Abraham, the same God Muslims worship.

Arab Christians have always called Him *Allāh*, and Arabic translations of the Bible use the same word. The Qur'an disputes what is said about Him: whether He has a son, whether He is three persons, whether a created being shares His divinity. It addresses Christians as people who worship God and attribute to Him things that are untrue of Him.

So when a Muslim says "your God is a false God", that Muslim departs from the Qur'an. The correct Muslim statement is: we worship the same God, and we believe you have attributed to Him things He has not revealed.

The argument has found a contradiction between the Qur'an and some Muslims' rhetoric. It has not found one inside the Qur'an.

## What a contradiction in the Qur'an would require {#contradiction}

This is the central problem with the argument's use of 29:46. It sets out to show that the Qur'an contradicts itself, and ends by showing that some Muslims contradict the Qur'an.

Those are different claims. The first would be a problem for Islam. The second is a problem for the Muslims concerned, and the remedy is for them to read the Qur'an more carefully.

For 29:46 to support the dilemma, it would have to say that Muslims must believe in the books Christians hold, as they hold them. It says Muslims must believe in what God sent down.

And for the "our God and your God" clause to support the dilemma, it would have to say that Christian doctrine about God is correct. It says only that the God both communities address is one.

## The same structure in the New Testament {#new-testament}

The New Testament treats the Hebrew Bible in a closely similar way. It affirms the Law and the Prophets as God's word. It also says that Jews who read them without faith in Christ misunderstand them:

> "But their minds were hardened. For to this day, when they read the old covenant, that same veil remains unlifted, because only through Christ is it taken away. Yes, to this day whenever Moses is read a veil lies over their hearts." -- 2 Corinthians 3:14–15 · ESV

Paul affirms the scripture and says its readers cannot see what it means. Christians do not regard that as a contradiction.

They regard it as a claim that the New Testament is the key to reading the Old. Muslims make the same claim about the Qur'an and all earlier scripture. The structure is identical; only the identity of the key differs.

## "But Muslims do say the Bible is corrupted" {#objection}

The argument's advocates will reply that this is a convenient reading, and that in practice Muslims do say, often and loudly, that the Bible has been corrupted. That is true, and Muslims should be honest about it.

Popular Muslim speech frequently compresses a careful position into a slogan. "The Bible is corrupted" is a slogan.

The careful position, held by the classical scholars and set out in the Qur'an's own words, is different. It says that God revealed the Torah to Moses and the Gospel to Jesus; that what their communities hold today contains parts of that revelation; that parts were lost, altered, misread or added; and that the Qur'an is the standard by which the two can be told apart.

A Muslim who holds that position can say, without contradiction, "I believe in what God revealed to you" and "I do not accept everything in the book you hold." Those two sentences are exactly what 29:46 and the Prophet's instruction together require.

The fair response to a slogan is to ask for the position behind it. Christians who present the dilemma should ask Muslims that question, and Muslims should be ready to answer it. When they do, the dilemma's choice between "you believe the Bible" and "you reject the Bible" disappears, because neither describes what the Qur'an teaches.

## How Muslims should use the verse {#use}

The verse's first instruction applies to Muslims who meet the Islamic Dilemma. They should not answer it with insults, or with blanket claims that the Bible is worthless and Christians worship a false god.

Both claims go beyond the Qur'an, and both hand the argument the opening it is looking for. They should begin where the verse tells them to begin: we believe in what God revealed to you and to us; our God and your God is one. And then they should explain what that belief does and does not include.

That is not a defensive retreat. It is the Qur'an's own opening move in a conversation with Christians, and it is a stronger position than either of the two the dilemma offers.

## A test for both sides {#test}

The verse also sets a standard Christians can hold Muslims to. If a Muslim says "your God is false" or speaks of the People of the Book with contempt, a Christian can fairly reply: your own scripture tells you to argue with me "in the best way", and to say that our God and your God is one. That is a fair reply, and Muslims should accept it.

## Conclusion {#conclusion}

Qur'an 29:46 tells Muslims to discuss with the People of the Book courteously, and to affirm belief in what God revealed to them and that the God of both communities is one. It does not tell Muslims to accept every book or doctrine Christians hold.

The Prophet's own guidance, "do not believe them and do not disbelieve them", shows how the verse was meant to be applied. The contradiction the argument finds lies between the Qur'an and careless Muslim speech.
