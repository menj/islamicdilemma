!lead The Islamic Dilemma pictures the question as "the Bible against the Qur'an", as if the Bible were the whole of what God revealed before Muhammad and the Qur'an had to accept it or reject it. The Qur'an's own picture of earlier revelation is larger than that. It speaks of scrolls given to Abraham that no Bible preserves, and it tells us what they taught. Their teaching cuts to the centre of the Christian case: whether God forgives without a sacrifice of blood. This article follows sections V.E and VII of [*Reconsidering the "Islamic Dilemma"*](https://www.academia.edu/166160379/Reconsidering_The_Islamic_Dilemma).

## The scrolls of Abraham and Moses {#scrolls}

Two short passages of the Qur'an name a category of revelation the dilemma never mentions. The first closes Sūrat al-Aʿlā:

::ayah Q 87:18–19 | إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَفِى ٱلصُّحُفِ ٱلْأُولَىٰ ۝ صُحُفِ إِبْرَٰهِيمَ وَمُوسَىٰ | This is indeed in the earlier scrolls, the scrolls of Abraham and Moses.

The second, in Sūrat al-Najm, asks whether a man has not been told what those scrolls contained, and then says:

::ayah Q 53:36–39 | أَمْ لَمْ يُنَبَّأْ بِمَا فِى صُحُفِ مُوسَىٰ ۝ وَإِبْرَٰهِيمَ ٱلَّذِى وَفَّىٰٓ ۝ أَلَّا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌۭ وِزْرَ أُخْرَىٰ ۝ وَأَن لَّيْسَ لِلْإِنسَـٰنِ إِلَّا مَا سَعَىٰ | Or has he not been told of what is in the scrolls of Moses, and of Abraham, who fulfilled his trust: that no bearer of burdens bears the burden of another, and that a human being has nothing but what he strives for?

The word *ṣuḥuf* is the plural of *ṣaḥīfah*, a written sheet or scroll: separate documents, not one bound book. Al-Ṭabarī explains "the earlier scrolls" as the revelations sent to the prophets before the Torah, and Ibn Kathīr records reports that Abraham received ten scrolls and Moses ten more before the Torah was given.[1]

## Three things these verses establish {#three}

**First, the Qur'an recognises revelation that the Bible does not preserve.** No Bible, Jewish or Christian, contains a scripture given to Abraham. Abraham appears in Genesis as a figure in a story, never as the recipient of a book. So when the Qur'an affirms "what came before", it cannot simply mean the Bible: it includes revelation the Bible never had. The dilemma's assumption, that the Qur'an's earlier scriptures map onto the Bible's table of contents, fails on the Qur'an's own words.

**Second, earlier revelation was many scriptures, given to many prophets.** The plural matters. The Qur'an speaks of scrolls, of the Torah, of the Psalms, of the Injīl, and of "messengers We have told you about, and messengers We have not" {Q 4:164}. Its view of earlier revelation does not commit it to any single collection of books. An argument that the Qur'an confirms "the Bible" must say which revelation, given to which prophet, in which form.

**Third, the Qur'an says what those scrolls taught.** And what they taught is the centre of the dispute between Islam and Christianity.

## "No bearer of burdens bears the burden of another" {#burden}

The principle the Qur'an attributes to Abraham and Moses is that every soul answers for itself. No one can carry another's sin, and no one is credited with another's striving. The Qur'an repeats it in four more places {Q 6:164 · 17:15 · 35:18 · 39:7}, which shows how central it is. It is the plain contradiction of vicarious atonement: the doctrine that one person's death pays for the sins of others.

The Qur'an presents this not as a new teaching but as the teaching of the oldest revelations, given to Abraham and Moses. And the Hebrew Bible, read on its own terms, agrees:

> "Fathers shall not be put to death because of their children, nor shall children be put to death because of their fathers. Each one shall be put to death for his own sin." -- Deuteronomy 24:16 · ESV

> "The soul who sins shall die. The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son. The righteousness of the righteous shall be upon himself, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon himself." -- Ezekiel 18:20 · ESV

Jeremiah says the same: "everyone shall die for his own iniquity" {Jer 31:30}. On the question of who bears a sin, the Hebrew prophets stand with the Qur'an.

## "Without the shedding of blood" {#blood}

The Christian doctrine of atonement rests heavily on one sentence in the Letter to the Hebrews:

> "Indeed, under the law almost everything is purified with blood, and without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness of sins." -- Hebrews 9:22 · ESV

Christian apologists who press the Islamic Dilemma treat this verse as the foundation of the case that Jesus had to die: without blood, no forgiveness; without the cross, no salvation. If the verse is true without qualification, Islam's teaching of forgiveness through repentance is empty.

But Hebrews is one voice in the Christian canon, and the Hebrew scriptures that Christians also hold as God's word say something different, again and again.

## What the Hebrew scriptures say instead {#hebrew-scriptures}

David, after his sin with Bathsheba, prays:

> "For you will not delight in sacrifice, or I would give it; you will not be pleased with a burnt offering. The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise." -- Psalm 51:16–17 · ESV

Samuel tells Saul that obedience counts for more than offerings:

> "Has the LORD as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the LORD? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to listen than the fat of rams." -- 1 Samuel 15:22 · ESV

Hosea gives God's own words: "For I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice" {Hos 6:6}. Jeremiah goes further, and has God say that when He brought Israel out of Egypt He did not command burnt offerings, but obedience {Jer 7:22–23}. Ezekiel promises that the wicked person who turns from sin "shall surely live", and that "none of the transgressions that he has committed shall be remembered against him" {Ezek 18:21–22}. Joel calls the people to "rend your hearts and not your garments", because God is "gracious and merciful" {Joel 2:13}.

None of these passages mentions blood. Each one ties forgiveness to repentance, obedience and God's mercy.

## The stories say it too {#stories}

The narratives agree with the prophets.

- **Nineveh.** In the book of Jonah, the people of Nineveh fast, put on sackcloth and turn from their evil, and God relents {Jonah 3:5–10}. No sacrifice is offered. They are forgiven because they repent.
- **David.** When Nathan confronts David over Uriah's murder and his adultery, David says, "I have sinned against the LORD", and Nathan answers, "The LORD also has put away your sin" {2 Sam 12:13}. Forgiveness follows confession, at once, without sacrifice.
- **The Day of Atonement.** Even the central rite of atonement in Leviticus involves two goats. One is sacrificed. The other, the goat "for Azazel", carries the sins of Israel into the wilderness and is not killed {Lev 16:20–22}.

## "It was all paid for in advance" {#in-advance}

The usual Christian reply is that every act of forgiveness in the Old Testament was granted on credit, paid for later by the blood of Christ. David and the people of Nineveh were forgiven, on this view, because of a death that had not yet happened.

That reply is a theological solution, not something the texts say. Psalm 51 does not mention a future payment, and neither does Jonah. Hebrews 9:22 says blood is required; it does not say "required later". The reply works only by reading the whole Hebrew Bible through one verse in Hebrews. Christians are free to read their canon that way. But it means the canon does not speak with one voice on this question, and the tension is resolved by interpretation, not by the texts.

## Why this matters for the dilemma {#dilemma}

The point is not that Christians cannot hold their doctrine. It is that the dilemma treats the Bible as a single, consistent standard against which the Qur'an is found wanting. On forgiveness, the most important question between the two faiths, the Bible itself holds two positions. One, in Hebrews, makes blood necessary. The other, across the Psalms, the Prophets and the narratives, ties forgiveness to repentance and mercy.

Christians resolve this by harmonising: reading the many passages in light of the one. Muslims are entitled to the same courtesy when they read the Qur'an as a whole. An argument that harmonises the Bible but refuses to let the Qur'an's verses be read together is using two standards. Applied evenly, either both books may be read as wholes, or neither may. The dilemma survives neither way.

And on the substance, the Qur'an's position is the older one. It says it is the teaching of Abraham and Moses. The Hebrew prophets confirm it. "No bearer of burdens bears the burden of another" is not a Muslim innovation against the Bible. It is the Bible's own prophets, and the scrolls before them, speaking. The Muslim understanding of sin and forgiveness is set out in full in [Sin, repentance and the mercy of God](/sin-and-repentance/).

!callout **In short.** The Qur'an speaks of scrolls given to Abraham and Moses that no Bible preserves, and says they taught that no soul bears another's burden {Q 53:38}. The Hebrew prophets agree, and the Psalms, Hosea, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Joel, Nineveh and David all show forgiveness without blood. "Without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness" {Heb 9:22} is one voice in the canon, not the Bible's only answer.
