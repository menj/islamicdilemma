title: Talking with Christians about the Islamic Dilemma
excerpt: A practical guide for Muslims: how to answer the argument, how to tell a sincere question from a hostile campaign, what to say, what never to say, and what to do when a Christian wants to become Muslim.

!lead The Islamic Dilemma is built to be used in conversation. It fits in a paragraph, it quotes the Qur'an, and it is often put to Muslims who have never studied the verses it relies on. This guide is for the Muslim on the receiving end. It explains how to prepare, how to answer, how to tell what kind of conversation you are in, and how to keep the argument where it belongs: on what the Qur'an actually says.

## Know the argument better than the person presenting it {#prepare}

Most Muslims who are unsettled by the dilemma are unsettled because they meet it unprepared. The cure is preparation, and it does not take long.

- Read the [argument as its advocates present it](/islamic-dilemma/), and then [the full Muslim response](/muslim-response/). Together they take less than an hour.
- Learn five verses by heart, with their context: 5:47, 5:48, 5:68, 10:94 and 6:115. Almost every version of the argument rests on them.
- Know the one word that carries the Muslim position: *muhaymin*, guardian {Q 5:48}. The Qur'an confirms what is true in the earlier scripture and judges what is not.
- Read one Gospel, preferably Mark, the shortest and earliest. A Muslim who has read the Gospels is not easily told what they say.

Preparation changes the conversation. The dilemma depends on the Muslim not knowing the passage the verses come from. Once you know it, you are the one asking questions.

## Ask rather than announce {#ask}

The most effective answer to the dilemma is usually a question, because it sends the other person back to the text. Some that work:

- When told the Qur'an commands Muslims to judge by the Gospel: "Can you read me 5:47? Who is the verse addressed to?" It is addressed to "the people of the Gospel", not to Muslims, and the next verse tells the Prophet to judge by the Qur'an.
- When told the Qur'an says the Gospel is intact: "Can we read 5:48 together? What does *muhaymin* mean?" The verse places the Qur'an as guardian over the earlier scripture.
- When told the Qur'an's Gospel is the New Testament: "Where does the Qur'an mention four Gospels, or Paul, or any letter?" It never does. It speaks of one Injil, given to Jesus {Q 5:46}.
- When told "none can change His words": "Does 18:27 speak about the Bible, or about what was revealed to the Prophet?" It says "recite what has been revealed to you".
- When told the Prophet doubted and was sent to Christians: "How does 10:94 end?" With "the truth has come to you from your Lord".

Each question can be checked in a minute. Each one moves the conversation from a summary of the Qur'an to the Qur'an itself.

## Two kinds of conversation {#two-kinds}

Not every Christian who raises the dilemma is doing the same thing, and it helps to know which conversation you are in.

**The sincere enquirer.** Many Christians raise the argument because they believe it and want to know what a Muslim says. They are neighbours, colleagues, classmates and friends. They listen to an answer, ask follow-up questions, and sometimes change their minds. With them, take your time. Read the passages together. Answer the question they asked. Invite them, plainly and without pressure, to consider Islam. The Qur'an gives the model:

::ayah Q 3:64 | قُلْ يَـٰٓأَهْلَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ تَعَالَوْا۟ إِلَىٰ كَلِمَةٍۢ سَوَآءٍۭ بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَكُمْ أَلَّا نَعْبُدَ إِلَّا ٱللَّهَ وَلَا نُشْرِكَ بِهِۦ شَيْـًۭٔا | Say: People of the Book, come to a word common between us and you: that we worship none but God and associate nothing with Him.

**The hostile campaigner.** Others use the dilemma not as a question but as a weapon. They present it in public, to an audience, often with misquotations, a tone of contempt, and claims about Muslims that no Muslim would recognise. For them the argument is part of a larger case: that Islam is a threat and Muslims are to be feared or shut out. You will recognise the pattern. The verses are quoted in part, the corrections are ignored, and the same claims return next week unchanged.

With the hostile campaigner, the goal is different. You are unlikely to persuade them, and you do not need to. You are speaking to the people listening.

- **Correct the record, precisely.** Name the verse that was misquoted and read the whole of it. Point out when English from one verse has been printed under the Arabic of another. The page on [errors in the case for the dilemma](/errors-in-case-for-dilemma/) lists the most common ones.
- **Name the tactic.** If the argument is being used to stir fear of Muslims, say so plainly. An argument about scripture does not need claims about Muslim neighbours to make its case.
- **Do not take the bait.** Mockery is meant to provoke a reply in kind. A Muslim who answers with insults gives the campaigner the clip he wanted. The Qur'an describes the servants of the Most Merciful as those who, "when the ignorant address them, say: peace" {Q 25:63}.
- **Keep the moral advantage.** The strongest reply to contempt is a calm, accurate answer that the audience can check.

::ayah Q 41:34 | وَلَا تَسْتَوِى ٱلْحَسَنَةُ وَلَا ٱلسَّيِّئَةُ ۚ ٱدْفَعْ بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ | The good deed and the bad are not equal. Repel evil with what is better.

## What never to say {#never}

Some Muslim answers lose the argument for us. They should be dropped.

- **"The Bible is completely corrupted."** The Qur'an does not say so. It says the earlier scripture contains God's ruling {Q 5:43}, guidance and light {Q 5:44, 46}, and the description of the coming prophet {Q 7:157}, alongside what was forgotten, concealed and distorted {Q 5:13–15}. The accurate position is stronger than the slogan.
- **"The Qur'an has never had a single variant."** It has canonical readings, recorded openly by Muslim scholars. Say what is true: [one written text, recited in ways the Prophet taught](/qiraat-and-preservation-of-quran/).
- **"The Gospel of Barnabas is the real Gospel."** It is a late medieval work. Leave it out of the conversation entirely.
- **"Christians believe God had a wife."** They do not, and saying so ends the discussion. The Qur'an's objection does not need a caricature: it denies that God takes a son in any sense {Q 19:88–92}.
- **Insults of any kind**, about Jesus, about Mary, about any prophet, or about the person in front of you. Jesus and Mary are honoured in Islam. A Muslim who mocks what Christians hold sacred has also mocked what the Qur'an honours.

## What to say instead {#instead}

A short answer that covers most of the dilemma, in words a Muslim can use:

!callout The Qur'an says God revealed the Injil to Jesus. It never says the New Testament is that Injil, and it never mentions four Gospels or any letter. It says the earlier scripture still contained God's truth, and also that parts were forgotten, concealed and altered. That is why it calls itself *muhaymin*, guardian over what came before. The dilemma offers two choices, wholly preserved or wholly lost. The Qur'an holds a third, and it says so in the same verse the argument quotes.

From there, let the other person choose the next step. If they want the detail, the [full response](/muslim-response/) and the Enrichment articles give it verse by verse.

## When the questions turn to Jesus {#jesus}

Conversations about the dilemma usually turn to the question underneath it: who Jesus was. Be ready for it. The Qur'an's answer is clear, and it is close to what the Gospels record of Jesus' own words: he called God "my God and your God" {John 20:17} and "the only true God" {John 17:3}, and in the Qur'an he says "worship God, my Lord and your Lord" {Q 5:117}. [Did Jesus claim to be God?](/did-jesus-claim-to-be-god/) reads the passages usually cited, in full.

Speak of Jesus with the honour the Qur'an gives him. Muslims believe in his virgin birth, his miracles, his message and his return. The disagreement is about his nature, and it is serious, but it is a disagreement between people who both love him.

## When a Christian wants to become Muslim {#embrace}

Sometimes the conversation ends somewhere neither side expected. If a Christian tells you they want to become Muslim:

- **Be clear and simple.** Becoming Muslim is bearing witness, sincerely, that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is His messenger. No ceremony and no intermediary is required. Explain what the words mean before they say them.
- **Do not rush them, and do not publicise it.** It is their decision, made before God, not a victory for you to announce.
- **Think about their circumstances.** A new Muslim may face difficulty with family, friends or work. Ask about their situation, and help them find a local mosque and Muslims who can support them.
- **Keep teaching gently.** Prayer, fasting and the rest are learned over time. The Prophet taught people step by step, and so should we.

The [invitation to Christians](/invitation-to-christians/) sets out what Islam asks, for anyone who wants to read it first.

## The rule that governs all of it {#rule}

::ayah Q 16:125 | ٱدْعُ إِلَىٰ سَبِيلِ رَبِّكَ بِٱلْحِكْمَةِ وَٱلْمَوْعِظَةِ ٱلْحَسَنَةِ ۖ وَجَـٰدِلْهُم بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ ۚ | Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good counsel, and argue with them in the way that is best.

Wisdom means knowing the verses and knowing the person. Good counsel means speaking to be understood, not to win. Arguing "in the way that is best" means meeting even a hostile argument with the strongest and most accurate answer, delivered without contempt. The Islamic Dilemma fails on the evidence. A Muslim's task is to show that, and to leave the listener free to follow the evidence where it leads.

===sidebar===
# Five verses to know
Q 5:47: Addressed to "the people of the Gospel"; a canonical reading makes it a statement of purpose.
Q 5:48: The Qur'an is *muhaymin*, guardian over the earlier scripture.
Q 5:68: "And what has been sent down to you" includes the Qur'an.
Q 10:94: A conditional that answers itself: "the truth has come to you".
Q 6:115: God's decrees cannot be overturned; it says nothing about manuscripts.
