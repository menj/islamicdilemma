!lead This site makes claims about the Qur'an, the Bible, the classical commentaries and the history of both texts. None of them should be taken on trust. This page lists the sources we rely on, explains what each one is and why it matters, and suggests how to use them to check what we say, or what anyone else says.

## The study behind this site {#study}

- Mohd Elfie Nieshaem Juferi, [*Reconsidering the "Islamic Dilemma"*](https://www.academia.edu/166160379/Reconsidering_The_Islamic_Dilemma) (Kuala Lumpur, 2026; 68 pages). The full academic case on which this site is built: the argument's four premises and the substitutions it depends on, the Qur'anic vocabulary of *muṣaddiq*, *al-Furqān* and *muhaymin*, the classical positions on *taḥrīf*, the history of the Gospel and of the biblical canon, the textual history of both Testaments, and the asymmetry of method in the argument itself. Every claim it makes is footnoted to primary sources and published scholarship. Read it free at Academia.edu.

## How to check a claim {#how-to-check}

Most disputes about scripture can be settled by following five steps, and they apply equally to this site and to the arguments it answers.

1. Find the verse in the original language, not only in translation. For the Qur'an, that means the Arabic; for the New Testament, the Greek.
2. Read the whole passage, at least ten verses either side. Many arguments depend on a verse read alone.
3. Compare two or three translations. If they differ, the difference is often where the argument lies.
4. See what the classical commentators said, especially the earliest ones. They often discussed the same objection centuries ago.
5. Check whether the claim matches what scholars of the text, including scholars of the other faith, have written.

The sources below are arranged to help with each of those steps.

## The Qur'an: text and translations {#quran}

- [Tanzil](https://tanzil.net) — A carefully verified Arabic text of the Qur'an, available in several scripts, with many translations for comparison. Useful for copying exact Arabic text and checking a verse's wording.
- [Quran.com](https://quran.com) — Arabic text with word-by-word glosses and a choice of English translations. The word-by-word view is the quickest way for a non-Arabic reader to see what each word in a verse means.
- [Corpus Coranicum](https://corpuscoranicum.de) — An academic project that documents early Qur'an manuscripts, variant readings and the historical context of the text. Useful for questions about the Qur'an's own transmission.
- M. A. S. Abdel Haleem, *The Qur'an: A New Translation* (Oxford, 2004) — A clear, modern English translation by a scholar of Arabic, widely used in universities.
- Muhammad Asad, *The Message of the Qur'an* (1980) — A translation with extensive notes, often discussing how verses relate to earlier scripture.

Translations on this site are our own unless noted. Where a point depends on a word's meaning, we give the Arabic so readers can check it.

## The text and readings of the Qur'an {#readings}

- Aḥmad ʿAlī al-Imām, *Variant Readings of the Qur'an: A Critical Study of Their Historical and Linguistic Origins* (1998). A study by a Muslim scholar of the seven *aḥruf*, the compilations of Abū Bakr and ʿUthmān, the companions' personal codices and the canonical readings. It shows how each reading was accepted or rejected, and is the best single book in English for answering claims that the readings show the Qur'an's text drifted. We draw on it in [The qirāʾāt and the preservation of the Qur'an](/qiraat-and-preservation-of-quran/).
- Ibn al-Jazarī, *al-Nashr fī al-Qirāʾāt al-ʿAshr*. The fifteenth-century work that set out the conditions for an accepted reading and documented the ten canonical readers.

## Classical commentary {#tafsir}

- al-Ṭabarī, *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān* — The earliest large commentary on the Qur'an, completed in the tenth century. Its value lies in the reports it preserves from the Prophet's companions and their students on what verses meant. We cite it on 5:44–48, 6:115, 10:94 and elsewhere.
- Ibn Kathīr, *Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm* — A fourteenth-century commentary that explains verses by other verses and by hadith. Widely available in English abridgement.
- Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, *Mafātīḥ al-Ghayb* — A twelfth-century commentary that discusses theological and philosophical objections in detail, including objections about earlier scripture.
- [Altafsir.com](https://www.altafsir.com) — Searchable Arabic texts of the major classical commentaries, with some English translations.
- [Al-Maktabah al-Shāmilah](https://shamela.ws) — The largest open library of classical Arabic works: the commentaries of al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, the hadith collections with their chapter headings, and the writings of Ibn Taymiyyah and others, page by page in their printed editions. We use it to check quotations in the original Arabic and to cite edition and volume.

## Hadith {#hadith}

- [Sunnah.com](https://sunnah.com) — The major hadith collections, including *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*, in Arabic and English, searchable by keyword. Hadith on this site are cited by the numbering used here (for example *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* 6463), so each can be looked up directly.
- [Al-Durar al-Saniyyah](https://dorar.net/hadith) — An Arabic hadith encyclopedia that gathers each report's sources and the gradings classical and modern scholars have given it. Useful for checking whether a report outside the two *Ṣaḥīḥ* collections is sound.
- [Daily Hadith Online](https://www.abuaminaelias.com/dailyhadithonline/) — Hadith in Arabic and English, arranged by topic, each with its source and grading. A good place to find what the Prophet said on a subject before checking the collections themselves.

## Classical writings on Christianity {#classical}

- Ibn Taymiyyah, *al-Jawāb al-Ṣaḥīḥ li-man Baddala Dīn al-Masīḥ* — A long fourteenth-century reply to a Christian treatise. It discusses whether the alteration of earlier scripture was of wording, of meaning or both. Partial English translation: Thomas F. Michel, *A Muslim Theologian's Response to Christianity* (1984).
- Ibn Ḥazm, *al-Fiṣal fī al-Milal wa-l-Ahwāʾ wa-l-Niḥal* — An eleventh-century survey of religions by an Andalusian scholar, with a detailed critique of the biblical text. Ibn Ḥazm held a stronger view of textual alteration than many others.
- ʿAbd al-Jabbār, *Tathbīt Dalāʾil al-Nubuwwah* — An eleventh-century work that includes an extended argument about how Christianity developed after Jesus. A section has been translated by Gabriel Said Reynolds and Samir Khalil Samir as *Critique of Christian Origins*.

These works show that the Muslim position on earlier scripture was worked out in detail centuries ago. They also show that classical Muslim scholars disagreed among themselves on some points, which is a sign of serious engagement, not confusion.

## The Bible: text and translations {#bible}

- [ESV Bible](https://www.esv.org) — The English Standard Version, a Protestant translation, quoted throughout this site because it is widely used by the Christians who present the Islamic Dilemma. Its footnotes openly mark passages that are missing from the earliest manuscripts.
- Nestle–Aland, *Novum Testamentum Graece* — The standard critical edition of the Greek New Testament, with an apparatus listing variant readings.
- [Codex Sinaiticus](https://codexsinaiticus.org) — Full images and a transcription of one of the two oldest near-complete Christian Bibles, from the fourth century.
- [Center for the Study of New Testament Manuscripts](https://www.csntm.org) — High-resolution images of Greek New Testament manuscripts.

## Scholarship on the New Testament's text {#text}

- Bruce M. Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* — The standard reference explaining, passage by passage, why the editors of the Greek text chose one reading over another. Written by a committed Christian scholar.
- Bruce M. Metzger and Bart D. Ehrman, *The Text of the New Testament* (4th edition, 2005) — An introduction to how the New Testament was copied, how errors and changes entered the manuscripts, and how scholars reconstruct the text.
- Bart D. Ehrman, *Misquoting Jesus* (2005) — A popular account of the same subject by a scholar who left evangelical Christianity. Useful as an introduction; Christian scholars have written replies to it, which are worth reading alongside it.

Muslims should use this scholarship carefully. It shows that the New Testament's text has a complex history, which supports the Qur'an's account. It does not show that the New Testament contains nothing of Jesus' teaching, and Muslims should not claim that it does.

## Early Christianity in Arabia {#arabia}

- On Tatian's *Diatessaron*, the second-century harmony of the Gospels used for centuries in Syriac-speaking churches, any good introduction to the history of the New Testament canon will help. Its long use shows that the Christians around Arabia did not all read the four Gospels in the form a modern reader knows.
- On the debates over which books belonged in the New Testament, the fourth-century *Church History* of Eusebius records which books were accepted, disputed or rejected in his time.

## Reading the Qur'an for the first time {#first-time}

Readers who have never read the Qur'an often find it harder to follow than they expect, because it is not arranged as a continuous story. It is arranged in surahs of very different lengths, roughly from longest to shortest, and each surah moves between subjects: God's signs in creation, the stories of earlier prophets, law, warnings of judgment and promises of mercy. A few suggestions help.

- Begin with a translation that has short notes, such as Abdel Haleem's, so that unfamiliar references are explained as they arise.
- Read a whole surah at a time, rather than isolated verses. For the subjects on this site, Sūrat Āl ʿImrān (3), Sūrat al-Māʾidah (5) and Sūrat Maryam (19) are the most relevant, because they discuss the People of the Book, the Gospel and Jesus at length.
- When a verse seems surprising, look it up in a commentary before drawing a conclusion. The commentators were answering many of the questions a modern reader asks.
- Read it aloud, or listen to it recited, at least sometimes. The Qur'an was revealed to be recited, and much of its force is in its sound.

## Reading the Bible as a Muslim {#bible-as-muslim}

Muslims who read the Bible to understand the Islamic Dilemma should do so with the same care they would want Christians to show the Qur'an. A few principles help here too.

- Distinguish the kinds of book in the Bible: law, history, poetry, prophecy, Gospels, letters, apocalypse. A letter of Paul is not the same kind of text as a saying of Jesus, and should not be read as one.
- Pay attention to the footnotes in modern translations. They often record where manuscripts differ, which is exactly the information the Islamic Dilemma leaves out.
- Read the sayings of Jesus in the Gospels closely. Muslims who do so usually find a great deal they recognise, which is what the Qur'an's account of the Injil would lead one to expect.
- Do not assume that every Christian reads a passage the way a particular apologist does. Christian traditions disagree among themselves, and a fair argument engages the strongest reading, not the weakest.

## Scholarship behind the study {#study-scholarship}

These are the main academic works on which [*Reconsidering the "Islamic Dilemma"*](https://www.academia.edu/166160379/Reconsidering_The_Islamic_Dilemma) draws, and which the articles built on it cite.

- Camilla Adang, *Muslim Writers on Judaism and the Hebrew Bible* (1996), and Thomas F. Michel, *A Muslim Theologian's Response to Christianity* (1984). Surveys of what classical Muslim authors said about the Bible's text.
- Gabriel Said Reynolds, "On the Qurʾānic Accusation of Scriptural Falsification (*taḥrīf*)" (2010), and Ryan Schaffner, "The Bible through a Qurʾānic Filter" (PhD, 2016). The Qur'an's charge, and how early Muslim writers applied it.
- Walid Saleh, *In Defense of the Bible* (2008). Al-Biqāʿī's defence of quoting the Bible in Qur'an commentary.
- Sidney H. Griffith, *The Bible in Arabic* (2013), and Nicolai Sinai, *The Qur'an: A Historical-Critical Introduction* (2017). The Christian scriptures in the Qur'an's world, and how the Qur'an engages them.
- Emanuel Tov, *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible* (4th ed., 2022). The standard reference on the Hebrew text and the Dead Sea Scrolls.
- Bruce M. Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* (2nd ed., 1994) and *The Canon of the New Testament* (1987). The variants, and the history of the canon.
- Sebastian P. Brock, *The Bible in the Syriac Tradition* (2006). The Peshitta and its 22-book New Testament.

## A note on using opponents' sources {#opponents}

We quote the Bible from a Christian translation and cite Christian scholars on its text deliberately. An argument about what Christians' own sources say is strongest when it uses those sources as Christians present them. We ask Christian readers to extend the same courtesy to the Qur'an: to read it in a translation Muslims recognise, with the commentaries Muslims use, before drawing conclusions about it.

We revise this list as we write new articles. Every source cited anywhere on the site should appear here; if you find one that does not, please let us know.

# Using these sources
Verses: Quote verses with their surah and verse number, in Arabic where possible, and check any English rendering against the original.
Hadith: Give the collection and, where possible, the number, so readers can find the report and its chain.
Bible: Name the translation. Different translations can change an argument.
